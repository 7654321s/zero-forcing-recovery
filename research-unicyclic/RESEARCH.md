# Minimum killing supports in connected unicyclic graphs

For an initial blue set $S$, write $t_G(S)$ for the number of synchronous zero-forcing rounds required to color $G$ blue, with value $\infty$ upon failure. Loss changes the initial color and preserves all vertices and edges. For each forcing set $B$, a minimum killing support is any minimum-cardinality set $H\subseteq B$ for which $t_G(B\setminus H)=\infty$. The property EveryMinimumSupport requires

$$
\max_{\substack{D\subseteq H\\ |D|\le k}} t_G(B\setminus D)
=
\max_{\substack{D\subseteq B\\ |D|\le k}} t_G(B\setminus D)
$$

for every forcing set $B$, every such $H$, and every integer budget $k\ge0$.

**Classification theorem.** A finite connected simple unicyclic graph satisfies EveryMinimumSupport if and only if it has one of the following forms.

1. A cycle with no attachments.
2. A triangle with only directly attached leaves, where each positive leaf count is at least two.
3. A triangle with directly attached leaves and leaf-count triple $(1,0,0)$ or $(1,1,0)$ up to permutation.

The proof uses structural reductions and independently checked finite certificates with extension arguments for unbounded path lengths. A separate census covers all 2,846 connected unicyclic isomorphism classes on 3 through 11 vertices. It gives 31 positive cases and 2,815 negative cases.

Lean 4.35.0 with the pinned Mathlib verifies the full pure-cycle theorem. For each simple cycle of order at least three, every initial forcing set and every minimum killing set have equal restricted and unrestricted worst times at every budget. The theorem includes infinity and transfers across graph isomorphism. The results for attached trees are mathematical arguments and finite checks at the scopes stated below.

## Pure cycles and the cyclic window argument

Fix a forcing set $B$ in $C_n$, $n\ge3$, and let $q$ be its minimum killing size. A set forces a cycle exactly when it contains an adjacent blue pair. It follows that the killing sets hit every edge with both endpoints in $B$. Both statements are formalized in CycleSeeds.lean.

Consider an arc whose two boundary vertices belong to active blue runs, that is, runs of at least two consecutive blue vertices. Each boundary can fill the nearest white vertex of the remaining arc in one round. Blue vertices between consecutive white vertices require no round. Thus the arc fills in at most half its number of white vertices, rounded up. If the open arc contains no adjacent blue pair, each force in the arc comes from one of the two advancing boundaries. Induction on rounds gives exactly the rounded half of its white count. A single active run may supply both boundaries through its two distinct endpoints. Lean verifies the global upper bound cycle_finishesBy_of_white_card_le and its all-blue robustness consequence cycle_full_blue_robust_upper.

Suppose first that $B$ is not full. Cut the cyclic order at an initially white vertex numbered zero. In each maximal blue run, start from its first vertex and form consecutive pairs. Leave the last vertex of each odd run unassigned. Call these pairs units. Each unit contains a blue edge, so a killing set hits each one. Within each run, selecting the even positions, counted starting at one, breaks every blue edge and takes one vertex per unit. Thus the number of units is $q$. Every minimum killing set $H$ chooses one vertex per unit and none outside them. In particular $B\setminus H$ contains no adjacent pair. Lean verifies this construction in cycle_exists_pair_packing_selector_at_white_zero by reusing the path's greedy packing. The cyclic order is still used for the time comparison.

Index the $q$ units cyclically and let $w_i$ count the original white vertices strictly between unit $i$ and the next unit. For $0\le k<q$ let $W_k$ be the largest sum of $k+1$ consecutive entries of the cyclic list $(w_i)$. The unrestricted and restricted worst times are both

$$
\left\lceil \frac{k+W_k}{2}\right\rceil.
$$

For the upper bound, at most $k$ units can contain a lost vertex. Consecutive intact units bound arcs with at most $k$ intervening units, at most $W_k$ original whites, and at most $k$ new whites. Apply the propagation observation to every such arc. If just one unit is intact, its two endpoints bound the same arc.

For the lower bound, choose the $k$ consecutive units bracketed by an arc with $W_k$ original whites and remove their $k$ chosen vertices from $H$. Every $H$-vertex inside the arc has now been removed. The remaining open arc has no internal adjacent pair. Absorb any blue extensions into the two boundary runs. This preserves all $k+W_k$ white vertices, so the exact case of the propagation observation gives the bound. When $k=0$, the arc is the ordinary gap between consecutive units. When $k=q-1$, the sole surviving unit bounds the selected arc on both sides.

Lean proves the equality using a comparison for each initially white target. It rotates the closest undamaged pair before that target to zero. The first undamaged pair after the target bounds its arc, or the initial pair bounds it across the cut. The arc contains at most $k$ packed pairs. The prefix of exactly $k$ packed pairs extends that arc and has exactly $k$ support points, all of which can be removed. The guarded-region upper bound and the white-chain lower bound use the same white count comparison as the window proof. The result is in cycle_everyMinimumSupport and cycle_everyMinimumSupportTime in CycleSupport.lean. The explicit maximum formula above is stated here as an ordinary mathematical consequence.

If $B$ is full, any loss of at most $k<q$ leaves an active blue run and at most $k$ whites, so completion takes at most $\lceil k/2\rceil$ rounds. For $1\le k<q$, delete the first $k$ members of $H$ in the numerical order around the cycle. Any two consecutive such members have a gap of at most two. A larger gap would leave adjacent vertices outside the edge cover $H$. The deleted vertices form a white chain with gaps of at most two. After $t$ rounds a chain with more than $2t$ initial white vertices still has a white vertex. In each round the next white on either side of an interior chain vertex blocks a source from that side, either by being that source or by being its second white neighbor. Induction gives the white-chain lower bound. Budget zero gives zero. In both the full and non-full cases, $k\ge q$ lets either adversary remove $H$ and attain infinity. Lean verifies the full-blue result in cycle_full_blue_support.

## Positive family with a triangle core

Let each triangle vertex have either no leaves or at least two leaves, and suppose at least one has leaves. Call a triangle vertex with leaves a supported vertex. A pair of leaves at the same vertex is a fort. Consequently a forcing set (B) has minimum killing size at most two. The only case with a nontrivial budget is minimum killing size two and budget one. In this case every leaf is blue, since each pair of sibling leaves must have both members in (B).

**One supported vertex.** The other two triangle vertices are adjacent twins, so they are both initially blue. If the supported vertex is also blue, the graph starts all blue and each single loss takes one round. Otherwise, removing any leaf or any bare triangle vertex gives two rounds. A surviving leaf colors the supported vertex in round one, and that vertex can then color the missing leaf or the missing bare vertex in round two. The missing vertex cannot turn blue in round one because the supported vertex is initially white and the other bare vertex has two white neighbors when a bare vertex was lost. All members of any minimum killing set are leaves or bare vertices, so the restricted and unrestricted worst times agree.

**Three supported vertices.** After any single loss all triangle vertices are blue by round one. After a leaf loss, its center can color that leaf in round one exactly when the whole triangle was initially blue. Otherwise the leaf becomes blue in round two. After a triangle-vertex loss there are no white leaves and the graph is blue by round one. No pair of triangle vertices is a killing set because the surviving leaves restore them, so every minimum killing set contains a leaf.

**Two supported vertices.** Write (z) for the bare vertex. In the all-blue case each single loss takes one round. Otherwise, after a leaf loss:

- If (z) is blue, the two supported vertices are blue by round one, and the missing leaf turns blue in round two. A missing supported vertex at time zero prevents the leaf from turning blue in round one.
- If (z) is white and both supported vertices were blue, the center without a missing leaf colors (z) in round one. The missing leaf turns blue in round two.
- If (z) is white and a supported vertex is initially white, both supported vertices are blue by round one, (z) becomes blue in round two, and the missing leaf becomes blue in round three. Neither center can color (z) in round one, and the center of the missing leaf sees (z) and the missing leaf at the start of round two.

These times are independent of which leaf was removed. A single triangle-vertex loss leaves all the leaves blue, so the supported vertices are restored by round one and (z) by round two. No pair of triangle vertices kills forcing for the same reason. Every minimum killing set therefore contains a leaf, attaining the unrestricted worst time.

For budget zero the choices agree, and for budgets at least the minimum killing size both choices attain infinity. This proves the property for the stated family at all budgets.

## The two small positive exceptions

For a triangle with one leaf, call its leaf-parent $a$ and the bare triangle vertices $b,c$. The twin pair $b,c$ is a fort, so the only nontrivial case is minimum killing size two and budget one. Both $b,c$ must be blue. At least one of $a$ and its leaf must also be blue, since either individual bare vertex alone cannot force. If the initial set is full, all singleton losses finish in one round. Otherwise it is a three-vertex set containing $b,c$ and either $a$ or its leaf. Each singleton loss then finishes in two rounds. When $a$ or its leaf is missing along with a bare vertex, restore $a$ first if it is white, or restore the bare vertex from its twin if $a$ is blue. When both $a$ and its leaf are white, the bare pair first restores $a$. These are all pairs of initial whites following a loss in this case, and neither pair of whites finishes in one round. Thus every minimum killing set gives the same worst time.

For a triangle with one leaf on each of two centers, call the centers $a,b$, the bare vertex $c$, and the respective leaves $p,q$. No singleton forces. The only nonforcing sets of two blue vertices are $\{a,b\}$, $\{a,p\}$, and $\{b,q\}$. This follows by the first available force. For example, in $\{a,q\}$ the leaf $q$ colors $b$, then $b$ colors $c$, then $a$ colors $p$. Every three-vertex blue set forces because the graph is twin-free and hence every pair of white vertices admits a first force. The only pairs of white vertices that both fill in one round are $\{a,b\}$ and $\{p,q\}$. For every other pair, at least one white vertex has no blue neighbor that sees only that white. For $\{a,c\}$ this vertex is $c$. For $\{a,p\}$ and $\{a,q\}$ it is the white leaf. For $\{c,p\}$ it is $p$. The other cases follow by exchanging $a,b$ and $p,q$.

- A forcing set of two blue vertices has killing size one. A three-vertex set has killing size two exactly when all three of its two-vertex subsets force. Up to the same exchange symmetry these sets are $\{c,p,q\}$ and $\{c,a,q\}$. In either set, exactly two of the singleton losses take three rounds and the third takes two. Every two-vertex killing set therefore contains a worst loss.
- A set of four blue vertices contains a nonforcing pair of blue vertices, so its killing size is two. For any fixed initially white vertex, at most one additional loss gives a pair of whites that fills in one round. Every minimum killing pair therefore contains a singleton loss that takes two rounds.
- The full initial set has killing size three. Any singleton loss takes one round and every pair of lost vertices takes at most two. Any three-element killing set contains a two-vertex loss other than $\{a,b\}$ or $\{p,q\}$, so the restricted and unrestricted worst times are both two at budget two.

Budget zero and budgets at least the minimum killing size agree for the same reasons as in the other positive families.

## Exclusion of the other leaf counts on a triangle

If at least one triangle vertex has exactly one leaf, the only triples besides the two exceptional ones are $(1,1,1)$ and $(1,a,b)$ with $a\ge2$ and $b\ge0$, up to permutation. In either case work at budget one and deadline two.

For $(1,1,1)$, let $x,y,z$ be the triangle vertices and let $B$ consist of $x,y$ and their two leaves. Let $H$ consist of those leaves. Removing both stalls the process. If only the leaf at $x$ is removed, $y$ colors $z$ in round one. The remaining white leaves fill in round two. The same holds with $x,y$ exchanged. If $x$ is removed instead, its surviving leaf restores $x$ in round one, $z$ turns blue in round two, and its leaf in round three. The loss of $y$ is symmetric. All single losses are finite, so $H$ is minimum and the two worst times differ.

For $(1,a,b)$, let $x,y,z$ have those leaf counts in order, and choose two leaves of $y$ as $H$.

- If $b\le1$, let $B$ consist of $x,z$ and all leaves of $y$. On losing a leaf of $y$, a surviving leaf restores $y$ in round one. The remaining white leaves fill in round two. If $x$ or $z$ is lost, $y$ becomes blue in round one and restores the lost center in round two. The leaf of $x$ remains white through round two and turns blue in round three. Any leaf of $z$ also turns blue by round three.
- If $b\ge2$, let $B$ consist of $x$ and all leaves of $y,z$. After a leaf loss, both $y,z$ are restored by round one, and the remaining white leaves turn blue in round two. If $x$ is lost, the centers $y,z$ turn blue in round one, $x$ in round two, and its sole leaf in round three.

Removing both members of $H$ leaves a permanent white fort, while all singleton losses finish. The no-loss time is at most two by monotonicity. This proves the required exclusion for all these triples. Together with the positive cases and the exhaustive checks of the two fixed small graphs, this settles the class of triangles with leaves attached directly to the triangle.

## Exclusion when a longer cycle has one attached path

For an (n)-cycle with (n\ge 5), attach a path of (p\ge1) new vertices at cycle vertex 0. Number the cycle vertices (0,1,\ldots,n-1) along the cycle. Take (B=\{n-1,0,1,2\}) and (H=\{0,2\}). Losing (H) leaves two nonadjacent blue cycle vertices of degree two, so the state is stalled.

- Losing (0) restores it in round one from (1). The (n-4) white vertices on the opposite arc are reached from (2) in round one and from (n-1) starting in round two, so the cycle finishes in (\lceil(n-3)/2\rceil) rounds. The attached path finishes in (p+1) rounds.
- Losing (2) leaves the consecutive triple (n-1,0,1). The white cycle interval finishes in (\lceil(n-3)/2\rceil) rounds, while the attached path finishes in (p) rounds.
- Losing (1) leaves only the front starting at (n-1) able to advance into the white arc. Its first (n-4) steps reach the neighbor of (2). Vertex (2) then colors (1) in round (n-3). Vertex (0) cannot start the attached path until the next round, hence completion at (n-3+p).
- Losing (n-1) leaves the consecutive triple (0,1,2). The front starting at (2) eventually restores (n-1), after which the attached path completes.

All single losses are finite, so (H) is minimum. Its one-loss worst time is (\max(p+1,\lceil(n-3)/2\rceil)), strictly below (n-3+p). The no-loss time is at most the one-loss times by monotonicity.

For (n=4), take (B) to consist of (0), its two cycle neighbors, and the whole attached path. Take (H) to be those two cycle neighbors. Losing both stalls. Losing one restores it and the fourth cycle vertex in round one. After losing (0), the attached path restores (0) in round one and the fourth cycle vertex turns blue in round two. A single loss on the attached path can be restored with the original blue cycle vertices in place. Thus the minimum killing size is two and the restricted time is one whereas the unrestricted time is at least two.

## Exclusion when a longer cycle has several leaves at one vertex

Let (n\ge4), attach at least two leaves at cycle vertex (0), and take (B) to consist of all leaves and (0) with its two cycle neighbors. Let (H) be two attached leaves. Losing (H) leaves a permanent white fort. With a single leaf loss, vertex (0) immediately restores that leaf while its cycle neighbors begin two fronts into the (n-3) initially white cycle vertices. Completion takes (\lceil(n-3)/2\rceil) rounds. Losing a cycle neighbor leaves an active adjacent pair and permitting propagation around the entire cycle. Losing (0) delays both fronts for one round until a leaf restores (0). Thus all singleton losses are finite, and this last loss takes one round more than any singleton loss in (H).

## Exclusion of shallow attachments with a pair of sibling leaves

Consider a cycle of order $n\ge4$ with leaves attached directly to arbitrary cycle vertices. Suppose some cycle vertex $a$ has at least two leaves. The single-attachment case was treated above. Now let $y\ne a$ be another vertex with a leaf. Except when $n=4$ and $y$ is opposite $a$, some cycle neighbor $x$ of $y$ lies outside the closed cycle neighborhood of $a$. Indeed, if $y$ is adjacent to $a$, its other cycle neighbor lies outside that neighborhood. Otherwise, both neighbors belonging to the neighborhood would mean that $a$ and $y$ have the same two cycle neighbors, which in a cycle is possible only for opposite vertices of $C_4$.

Take $B=V\setminus\{x\}$ and let $H$ be two leaves at $a$. With budget one restricted to $H$, $x$ and any lost leaf both turn blue in one round. The lost leaf is forced by $a$, whose two cycle neighbors remain blue. The vertex $x$ can be forced by either of its cycle neighbors, as neither is $a$. Without the support restriction, removing a leaf at $y$ postpones its recovery beyond round one: at time zero its only neighbor $y$ sees both $x$ and that leaf white.

The set $H$ kills forcing. To see that it is minimum, first consider any singleton leaf loss. Some cycle neighbor of $x$ other than the center of that leaf forces $x$ in round one. Once the whole cycle is blue, the lost leaf can recover. For a singleton cycle-vertex loss all leaves are blue. If $n\ge5$, at least one adjacent blue pair remains on the cycle because deleting two vertices leaves more than $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ blue vertices. The pair propagates on the cycle with all pendant leaves already blue. If $n=4$, $x$ is opposite $a$. Losing a cycle vertex other than $a$ leaves an adjacent blue pair. Losing $a$ leaves two opposite white cycle vertices, and a leaf of $a$ restores it in round one, after which the cycle completes. Thus every singleton loss is finite and the restricted deadline of one fails globally.

It remains to treat a four-cycle with attachments only at two opposite vertices $a,c$, where $a$ has at least two leaves. Write $b,d$ for the bare vertices. Let $H=\{b,d\}$, a twin pair, and take $B$ to contain $a,b,d$ and all leaves of $a$. If $c$ has one leaf, leave that leaf white. Losing $b$ or $d$ first fills the lost vertex and $c$ in parallel, then fills the leaf of $c$. Losing $a$ instead forces $a$, then $c$, then its leaf in three rounds. Any single leaf loss at $a$ is recovered in one round, concurrently with filling $c$, and the remaining leaf is filled in the second round. Thus the restricted deadline is two and the loss of $a$ defeats it.

If $c$ has two or more leaves, add all of them to $B$. Losing either bare vertex and recovering $c$ take one round, so the restricted deadline is one. Losing a leaf of $c$ delays restoring that leaf to round two because $c$ starts white. All single losses finish: surviving leaves restore their centers in round one, a lost bare vertex is restored by $a$ in round one, and a lost leaf is restored once its center is blue with both cycle neighbors blue. In both cases $H$ kills forcing, completing the exclusion argument.

## A two-white-vertex obstruction for deeper attachments

Let $G$ be a connected simple graph. Call distinct vertices $x,z$ twins if every vertex outside $\{x,z\}$ is adjacent to both or to neither. This includes adjacent and nonadjacent twins. Suppose the following three conditions hold.

1. A vertex $a$ has two distinct leaf neighbors.
2. A nonleaf vertex $x$ other than $a$ is not adjacent to $a$ and has no twin.
3. Some neighbor $y$ of $x$ has a leaf neighbor.

Then $G$ fails EveryMinimumSupport. Take $B=V\setminus\{x\}$, let $H$ be two leaves of $a$, and use budget one. If one vertex $z$ is lost from $B$, the white set is $\{x,z\}$. Since $x$ has no twin, some blue vertex sees exactly one of the two whites. The remaining white vertex, if any, has a blue neighbor by connectivity. All singleton losses therefore finish. Losing both leaves of $H$ makes those two leaves permanently white. Consequently $H$ has minimum size two.

The no-loss case and either singleton loss in $H$ finish within one round. If a leaf of $a$ is lost, $a$ can restore it since $a$ is not adjacent to $x$. Also, any neighbor of $x$ can force $x$ because the lost leaf has no neighbor other than $a$. Now lose a leaf of $y$ instead. It belongs to $B$ because $x$ is a nonleaf. The only possible source $y$ sees both $x$ and that leaf white, so the leaf stays white after round one. A neighbor of $x$ other than $y$ forces $x$ in round one, and the lost leaf then turns blue in round two. This gives a one-round restricted deadline and a two-round unrestricted witness.

A nonleaf vertex with a leaf neighbor has no twin. If a potential twin is not that leaf, the leaf distinguishes the two vertices. If the potential twin is the leaf, a second neighbor of the nonleaf distinguishes the pair. In a unicyclic graph with cycle length at least five, every nonleaf is twin-free even without a leaf neighbor. Adjacent nonleaves that are twins would create a triangle. Nonadjacent nonleaves that are twins would share at least two neighbors and create a four-cycle.

This gives a full classification within the connected leafy unicyclic graphs, where every nonleaf has at least two leaf neighbors. The subgraph induced by the nonleaves is connected and contains the unique cycle. If it is not complete, take a shortest path between nonadjacent vertices and use its first three vertices as $a,y,x$. All three have leaf neighbors and the obstruction applies. If the subgraph is complete, unicyclicity forces it to be a triangle. The positive triangle argument then applies. Thus the leafy unicyclic graphs satisfying EveryMinimumSupport are precisely the triangles with at least two leaves at each vertex.

Jacob's [Well-failed graphs, Definition 3.1 and Theorem 3.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.19357v1#S3) uses the same leafy condition and proves the static well-failed property throughout this class. The argument here identifies which leafy unicyclic graphs also preserve the worst propagation time when losses are restricted to minimum killing sets.

## Complete classification when the unicyclic graph has twins

**Theorem.** A finite connected simple unicyclic graph with at least one pair of twins satisfies EveryMinimumSupport exactly in these cases.

1. The graph is a pure triangle or a pure four-cycle.
2. The graph is a triangle with one attached leaf.
3. The graph is a triangle with leaves attached directly to its vertices, with at least one attachment, and every positive attachment count is at least two.

For the proof, delete the cycle edges and call each resulting tree rooted at a cycle vertex an attachment tree. A site is a cycle vertex whose attachment tree contains another vertex. Every nonempty attachment at a site has a leaf parent within its own tree, possibly the root. An attachment is deep if there is a vertex at distance at least two from the cycle. A deep attachment has a nonleaf vertex off the cycle with a leaf neighbor, obtained as the parent of a deepest leaf.

**General certificate.** Let $H=\{u,v\}$ be a twin pair in any connected graph with at least three vertices. Let $x$ be twin-free and outside $H$. Start with $B=V\setminus\{x\}$. Every singleton loss finishes in at most two rounds, since there is a blue vertex that sees just one member of $\{x,z\}$ for every singleton loss $z$. The other white vertex then has a blue neighbor by connectivity. The twin pair $H$ is permanent when white, so it is a minimum killing set of size two. To obtain a counterexample, it suffices to arrange that both white pairs $\{x,u\}$ and $\{x,v\}$ fill in one round, and that some other pair $\{x,z\}$ takes two. An initially white vertex fills in round one exactly when it has an outside neighbor that sees it as its unique white neighbor.

In particular, a sibling pair of leaves at $a$ and a singleton leaf $x$ at a different parent $y$ always give such a certificate. The leaf $x$ has no twin, because $y$ has no other leaf and has degree at least two. When a member of $H$ is lost, $a$ can restore it and $y$ can restore $x$ in the same round. Losing $y$ instead takes two rounds, since $x$ can only be restored after $y$ is blue. Also, if there is a different leaf parent $x$ not adjacent to $a$, it has no twin. The same certificate applies with $z$ a leaf of $x$. A leaf at $x$ restores $x$ when losing a member of $H$, but that leaf cannot restore itself in round one when it is the vertex lost.

**Twins among nonleaves.** Adjacent nonleaf twins must be two degree-two vertices of the unique triangle. They have a common neighbor by the twin condition and their nonleaf degree. A second common neighbor would create another cycle. All attachments are therefore at the remaining triangle vertex. If an attachment is deep, take $x$ to be an off-cycle leaf parent. Its leaf restores it while the surviving twin restores the lost twin in the same round. Losing that leaf while $x$ is white takes two rounds. If there is no deep attachment, the graph is a pure triangle, a triangle with one leaf, or a triangle with a sibling group.

Nonadjacent nonleaf twins must be opposite degree-two vertices of the unique four-cycle. They have at least two common neighbors, and three would create more than one four-cycle. If there is an attachment, choose a leaf parent $x$, which is twin-free. At least one common neighbor of $u,v$ is different from $x$ and not adjacent to $x$. If $x$ is one of the two common neighbors on the cycle, the other is opposite to it on the cycle. If $x$ is off the cycle, adjacency to both common neighbors would create a second cycle. This neighbor restores either lost twin in one round, while $x$ is restored by a leaf. Losing a leaf of $x$ instead takes two rounds. With zero attachments the graph is a pure four-cycle. A leaf and nonleaf cannot be twins, since the leaf has one neighbor and sharing all outside neighbors would force the nonleaf to have the same degree.

**Sibling leaves on a cycle of length at least four.** Let $a$ be the parent of a sibling pair and $r$ the site of its attachment tree. If another site is present, choose a leaf parent $p$ in that other attachment tree. If $p$ is not adjacent to $a$, use the general certificate with $x=p$ and the delay at a leaf of $p$. If $p$ is adjacent to $a$, then both are cycle vertices at adjacent sites. Let $x$ be the other cycle neighbor of $p$. This vertex is not adjacent to $a$. For cycle length at least five it is twin-free, since any twins among nonleaves would create a triangle or four-cycle. In a four-cycle its only possible twin would be the opposite vertex $a$, which has additional leaf neighbors. Thus the earlier two-white-vertex obstruction applies with $y=p$.

If there is only one site $r$, color blue all off-cycle vertices and the three cycle vertices consisting of $r$ and its two cycle neighbors. Use the sibling pair anywhere in that attachment tree as $H$. A singleton loss in $H$ is restored in round one, while two fronts from the two blue cycle neighbors fill the other $n-3$ cycle vertices in $\lceil(n-3)/2\rceil$ rounds. Removing $r$ instead lets an off-cycle neighbor restore $r$ in round one, but both cycle fronts wait during that round. A singleton loss of another initially blue cycle vertex leaves an active adjacent pair and all off-cycle vertices blue, so the cycle finishes. A singleton loss off the cycle is restored immediately, since all its other neighbors are blue and the site has both cycle neighbors blue. This proves the killing pair is minimum and exhibits a one-round gap.

**Sibling leaves on a triangle.** If there is a singleton leaf at any other parent, the general certificate already applies. If all leaf parents have at least two leaves and there is a deep attachment at the only site, use the adjacent bare twins. If there is a deep attachment and at least two sites, choose its off-cycle leaf parent $a$ and a leaf parent $x$ in another tree. These are nonadjacent and give the certificate. In the remaining case attachments are all leaves directly at the triangle, with every nonzero count at least two. The positive triangle argument applies.

Finally, a pair of twins that are both leaves must have the same parent. This exhausts the kinds of twins in a connected unicyclic graph and proves the theorem.

## Two singleton leaves on a longer cycle

**Proposition.** A simple cycle of length $n\ge4$ with exactly two added leaves at distinct cycle vertices fails EveryMinimumSupport. The proof gives a minimum killing set of size two and uses budget one.

Number the cycle $0,1,\ldots,n-1$ in order, put the leaf sites at $0$ and $d$, and take $1\le d\le\lfloor n/2\rfloor$. Write $u$ for the leaf of $0$ and $v$ for the leaf of $d$. The following choices use all the vertices listed in $B$ and no others. The last two columns give the worst restricted time and the time of losing just $z$.

| Case | $B$ | $H$ | $z$ | Restricted time | Loss of $z$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $d=1$ | $\{1,2,3,u\}$ | $\{1,3\}$ | $2$ | $\max(2,\lceil(n-2)/2\rceil)$ | $n-1$ |
| $n=2d$, $d\ge2$ | $\{0,1,n-1,u\}$ | $\{n-1,u\}$ | $0$ | $d$ | $d+1$ |
| $d=2$, $n\ge5$ | $\{1,2,3,u\}$ | $\{2,u\}$ | $3$ | $\max(3,n-3)$ | $n-1$ |
| $n=2d+1$, $d\ge3$ | $\{1,2,n-1,u\}$ | $\{2,u\}$ | $n-1$ | $d+1$ | $d+2$ |
| $d\ge3$, $n\ge2d+2$ | $\{1,2d-1,2d,u\}$ | $\{1,u\}$ | $2d-1$ | $n-d-1$ | $n-d$ |

In these calculations a front advances by one white vertex per round along a bare path, provided its current source has a blue neighbor behind it. It can pass an isolated blue vertex without paying an additional round for that vertex. A blue leaf can restore its parent in one round. A blue cycle vertex with a white leaf and a white cycle neighbor is blocked until one of them becomes blue. These facts follow by inspecting the unique-white-neighbor rule at the start of each round. In every case the no-loss time is bounded by a singleton-loss time by monotonicity.

**Adjacent sites.** Removing $H$ leaves $2$ and $u$ blue. The leaf restores $0$ in round one, and the process stalls with $0,2,u$ blue. Removing $1$ alone restores $0,1$ in round one. The arc from $3$ to $0$ begins receiving a front from $3$ in round one and from $0$ in round two. The leaf $v$ is restored in round two. Removing $3$ instead restores $0,3$ in round one, and the two fronts into the remaining $n-4$ ring vertices start in round two. That takes $1+\lceil(n-4)/2\rceil$ rounds in total, with $v$ restored in round two. This gives the stated restricted time. Removing $2$ leaves only the restoration of $0$ in round one. The reverse front from $0$ traverses the $n-4$ vertices down to $4$, then activates the already blue $3$ to restore $2$. If $n=4$, $3$ is active immediately after $0$ is restored. In either event $2$ becomes blue in round $n-2$ and $v$ in round $n-1$. Removing $u$ leaves the front from $3$ to traverse the arc to $0$, after which both leaves recover. Thus every singleton loss finishes.

**Opposite sites.** Removing $H$ leaves the adjacent pair $0,1$. The front from $1$ reaches $d$, while $0$ remains blocked by $u$ and $n-1$. The front stops at $d$, which sees $v$ and $d+1$ white. For the loss of $n-1$ alone, the blue leaf $u$ allows $0$ to start the reverse front, and $1$ starts the forward front in the same round. Both reach the other site or its last white neighbor by round $d-1$. The leaf $v$ is restored in round $d$. For the loss of $u$, the two fronts from $1,n-1$ run immediately while $0$ restores $u$, giving the same round $d$ for $v$. If $0$ is lost, both fronts wait one round for $u$ to restore $0$, so $v$ takes $d+1$ rounds. The loss of $1$ is symmetric to the loss of $n-1$.

**Distance two, with $n\ge5$.** Removing $H$ leaves $1,3$ blue with two white neighbors each, so it stalls. If $2$ alone is lost, $u$ restores $0$ in round one, then $1$ restores $2$ in round two. The two fronts into the arc $4,\ldots,n-1$ start in rounds two and three, and $v$ is restored in round three. The time is $\max(3,1+\lceil(n-3)/2\rceil)$. If $u$ alone is lost, $1$ restores $0$ and $2$ restores $v$ immediately. The front from $3$ advances to $n-1$, and only then can $0$ restore $u$. The time is $\max(2,n-3)$. The larger of these is $\max(3,n-3)$. For the loss of $3$, round one restores $0$. The site $2$ remains blocked by $v$ and $3$, so only the reverse front from $0$ traverses the $n-3$ ring vertices through $3$. It reaches $3$ in round $n-2$ and $v$ fills in round $n-1$. For the loss of $1$, $u$ restores $0$ and the front from $3$ eventually reaches $n-1$. The site $0$ can then restore $1$, permitting $2$ to restore $v$.

**Odd and nearly opposite sites.** Removing $H$ leaves the stalled coloring with $1,n-1$ blue. If $2$ alone is lost, $u$ restores $0$ in round one, and both fronts cover their $d-1$ remaining vertices before $v$ turns blue in round $d+1$. For the loss of $u$, the front from $2$ reaches $d$ in $d-2$ rounds, while $1$ restores $0$ in round one. The reverse front from the already blue $n-1$ then reaches $d+1$ in round $d$, so restoring $v$ takes $d+1$ rounds. The leaf $u$ itself is recovered in round two. Removing $n-1$ leaves the reverse front from $0$ starting in round two after $u$ restores $0$. This front has $d$ whites through $d+1$, and $v$ is restored in round $d+2$. Removing $1$ lets $u$ restore $0$, and $0$ restores $1$ in round two with $n-1$ and $u$ blue. Both fronts eventually reach the site $d$.

**Separated sites with $n\ge2d+2$.** Put $a=2d-1$ and $b=2d$. When $H$ is lost, the sole initially blue pair $a,b$ eventually reaches the sites $d,0$ from the longer arc. Both sites remain blocked by their own leaves and a white neighbor on the shorter arc. If just $1$ is lost, $u$ restores $0$ in round one. The front from $b$ reaches $n-1$ in round $n-2d-1$, which is at least one. The site $0$ then starts the front through $1$ to $d-1$, which is reached in round $n-d-2$. The other front has reached $d$ by round $d-1$. The leaf $v$ therefore fills in round $n-d-1$. If just $u$ is lost, the fronts from $a,b$ reach $d,0$ in rounds $d-1,n-2d$, respectively. The now blue $0$ activates the already blue $1$, which advances to $d-1$ by round $n-d-2$. This gives the same time $n-d-1$, since $u$ itself is restored one round after $0$. If $a$ is lost, both fronts wait for $u$ to restore $0$ in round one. From $1$ the forward front reaches $d$ in round $d-1$. The reverse front from $0$ traverses the $n-d-1$ vertices down to $d+1$, exactly one of which, $b$, is already blue. Thus it reaches $d+1$ in round $n-d-1$ and $v$ fills in round $n-d$. Losing $b$ instead of $a$ also leaves an isolated blue vertex on the longer arc, so the same fronts complete the coloring.

All five constructions make $H$ a minimum killing set because loss of $H$ fails permanently and all singleton losses terminate. In each row of the table the loss of $z$ exceeds the restricted deadline.

## One attachment site and some separated sites

**Theorem.** A connected unicyclic graph with exactly one attachment site and cycle length at least four fails EveryMinimumSupport, for every nontrivial rooted tree attached at that site. At cycle length three the graph satisfies the property exactly when every attached vertex is a leaf adjacent to the site. The triangle assertion follows from the bare adjacent twin pair and the triangle positive proofs above.

The general long-cycle construction also applies to some graphs with several sites. Let $S$ be the set of sites on a cycle of length $n\ge4$. Suppose $S$ is independent and some $r\in S$ has cyclic distance at least three from every other site. Color all off-cycle vertices blue and let the initial cycle blues be

$$
Q=S\cup\{r-1,r+1\},
$$

where $r-1,r+1$ denote its preceding and following cycle vertices. Let $w=n-|Q|$. If $w$ is even, require a cycle vertex outside $Q$ with no neighbor in $Q$, add it to the initial blue cycle set, and redefine $w$ as one less. Thus $w$ is odd. Choose the killing pair $H=\{r-1,r+1\}$. When both are removed, the remaining ring blues are independent and the off-cycle vertices are already blue. There is no force. If one member of $H$ is removed, the ring blues contain exactly one active consecutive run. The initially white ring vertices all occupy its one remaining gap, with isolated blue vertices between some whites. The two ends fill the gap in $\lceil(w+1)/2\rceil=(w+1)/2$ rounds.

If $r$ is lost, the remaining cycle blues are independent. An off-cycle neighbor restores $r$ in round one and there is no other change on the cycle during that round. The two ends of the original active run then fill the $w$ ring whites. Completion takes $1+\lceil w/2\rceil=(w+3)/2$. The extra isolated blue vertex, if used, is another possible loss and leaves the original active run. If another site is lost, it restores in the first round by an off-cycle neighbor, leaving the colors of all other original seeds unchanged. An off-cycle singleton loss is restored immediately from an off-cycle neighbor unless it is a direct leaf of a site. A lost direct leaf at $r$ is restored immediately. At another site it can block a front, but the fronts advancing in both directions from $r$ eventually fill the two ring neighbors of that site. The missing leaf then restores. Thus all single losses finish and $H$ is minimum.

With one site and even $n$, the construction uses $w=n-3$, already odd. For odd $n\ge7$, take the third vertex after $r$ as the extra isolated blue, changing $w$ to $n-4$. This leaves only the five-cycle.

For $C_5$, write $0$ for the site. If any sibling pair exists, the delayed start proof for a sibling pair at the sole site or anywhere in its attachment tree already applies. Otherwise use initial cycle blues $\{0,1,2,3\}$ and support $H=\{1,3\}$. Losing both leaves $0,2$ on the cycle, which cannot force even when the entire attachment is blue. With either single loss from $H$, all cycle whites fill in one round. If a leaf adjacent to $0$ exists, color the whole attachment blue and take that leaf as the slow loss. The site sees both the leaf and $4$ white at time zero. The leaf waits until round two. Every singleton loss in this construction leaves two whites including $4$, which is twin-free, so each singleton loss finishes.

If there is no direct leaf at $0$, choose an off-cycle leaf $u$ with parent $a$ and color the entire attachment except $u$ blue. Losing either member of $H$ still leaves $a$ and its other neighbors blue, so $u$ is restored in round one. Losing $a$ instead delays $u$ past round one. Any singleton loss on the ring finishes all cycle vertices by round one, after which $u$ restores. For any other off-cycle singleton loss the cycle finishes by round one by the force from $3$ to $4$. At most two whites then remain, one of which, if both remain, is $u$. It is twin-free because there are no sibling leaves. Both therefore finish. This proves minimality of $H$ and the one-round restricted deadline for every five-cycle case.

## Two sites with a single path at one of them

Suppose a ring of length $n\ge4$ has exactly two sites. One site has a single pendant path of length $L\ge1$. At the other site, called $0$, the attached tree can be arbitrary. Let $p$ be the shorter cyclic distance between sites. Every graph in this class fails EveryMinimumSupport if $p=1$, if $p=2$, or if the two cyclic distances differ by at most one. It also fails in the remaining distance cases under one of these conditions at site $0$.

- There are at least two off-cycle neighbors of $0$ and none is a leaf.
- Its sole off-cycle neighbor is a nonleaf and has no leaf child in the rooted attachment tree.

In every construction below, color the entire off-cycle tree at site $0$ blue and the entire off-cycle path at the other site white. A loss on the cycle leaves the blue tree intact, so it can restore $0$ in round one if necessary. The path site cannot force along the ring while one ring neighbor and the first path vertex are white. Once that site and both of its ring neighbors are blue, the white path takes exactly $L$ further rounds.

| Distance case | Initial ring blues | Killing pair | Slow loss | Restricted deadline |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $p=1$ | $1,2,3$ | $1,3$ | $2$ | $\max(1+L,\lceil(n-2)/2\rceil)$ |
| $n=2p$ | $0,1,n-1$ | $1,n-1$ | $0$ | $p-1+L$ |
| $p=2$, $n\ge5$ | $1,2,3$ | $1,2$ | $3$ | $n-3+L$ |
| $n=2p+1$, $p\ge3$ | $0,1,n-1,n-3$ | $1,n-1$ | $0$ | $p-1+L$ |

In these four rows label the path site $p$ along the cycle from $0$. If $p=1$, losing the killing pair leaves only $2$ and the restored $0$ on the cycle, which stalls. Losing either member instead restores both $0,1$ or both $0,3$ in round one. The remaining ring whites occur in one gap with fronts starting no later than round two. The path starts immediately after round one, yielding the displayed restricted deadline. If $2$ is lost instead, the first round restores only $0$ and the only following front goes backward from $0$. After $n-4$ whites along that arc it activates the initially blue $3$ to restore $2$ in round $n-2$. The path finishes in $n-2+L$ rounds. If an off-cycle vertex in the blue tree is lost, the original pair at $2,3$ can send its clockwise front all the way to $0$. After that the whole ring is blue and the one lost tree vertex and the white path can be restored.

For opposite sites, losing either support vertex leaves one active pair containing $0$. Its two fronts make both neighbors of the opposite site and the site itself blue by time $p-1$. With both neighbors of $0$ initially blue and $0$ then lost, the two fronts wait one round for $0$ to restore, giving time $p+L$ for completion. In the odd nearly opposite case the isolated blue vertex $n-3$ removes one white from the longer side, giving the same two arrival times. This extra vertex is not adjacent to either $1$ or $n-1$, so the loss of $0$ still delays both fronts a full round. In both cases losing the two support vertices leaves $0$ and at most one isolated additional blue on the cycle. With an off-cycle loss instead, $0$ and both of its cycle neighbors are blue, so the missing tree vertex can be restored without interrupting the ring fronts. Losing the extra isolated blue, if present, leaves the two original fronts.

At distance two with $n\ge5$, losing both $1,2$ leaves the restored $0$ and $3$ isolated. Losing $1$ leaves $2,3$ blue on the cycle at time zero. When $0$ has been restored and the clockwise front from $3$ has reached $n-1$ in round $n-4$, the site $0$ can restore $1$ in round $n-3$. The path then takes $L$ more rounds. Losing $2$ instead restores $0$ in round one and $2$ in round two. The remaining ring gap takes at most $1+\lceil(n-3)/2\rceil$ rounds in total, and the path takes $2+L$ in total. Both are bounded by $n-3+L$. Losing $3$ blocks the site $2$ and lets only the reverse front from the restored $0$ fill the other side of the ring. The time is $n-2+L$. If the loss is off the cycle, the original three consecutive ring vertices include the path site and both its neighbors. Both ring fronts can start immediately and the path site presents no obstruction.

For the remaining unequal arcs, orient the numbers so that the path site is at $D=n-p\ge p+2$ with $p\ge3$. Use cycle blues $\{1,2,3\}$, support $H=\{1,3\}$, and slow loss $2$. If $D=p+3$, include the extra isolated blue vertex $n-2$. Losing both members of $H$ leaves the restored $0$, $2$, and possibly $n-2$ independent. For each single ring loss under comparison, $0$ is restored in round one. The short-side front from $0$ reaches the neighbor $D+1$, ready to force, in round $R=p$ without the extra blue and round $R=p-1$ with it. In the long direction, the corresponding round $F$ at $D-1$ is $D-4$ on loss of $1$, $D-3$ on loss of $3$, and $D-2$ on loss of $2$. The last case counts the initial wait for $0$ and skips the already blue $3$. The path can start after round $\max(F,R,1+\min(F,R))$. The two ring fronts advance independently until the path site is blue with both ring neighbors blue. If $n-2$ is already blue and equals $D+1$, its readiness time counts the arrival of its supporting blue neighbor. This still gives the same path start because the long-side round is at least the short-side readiness time in that case.

If $D=p+2$, the restricted deadline is $p+L$ and the slow time is $p+1+L$. If $D=p+3$, the extra isolated blue ensures those same values. If $D\ge p+4$, the two times are $D-3+L$ and $D-2+L$. Losing the extra blue instead leaves the initial active run intact. If an off-cycle vertex of the blue tree is lost, the conditions at $0$ ensure that $0$ eventually becomes blue with no off-cycle neighbor still white. With at least two first neighbors, one unaffected branch can restore $0$, and a lost first neighbor has a child that can restore it independently. With one first neighbor, a loss of that neighbor or of one of its children is restored from further down the tree because the affected vertex is not a leaf. A deeper loss does not prevent the first neighbor from restoring $0$. The ring and the white path can then finish. Any remaining single white vertex in the blue tree is restored as well.

All singleton losses therefore finish in the stated scope. The displayed killing pairs are minimum and the slow loss misses the restricted deadline in every row and in the unequal-arcs construction.


## Pendant paths meeting at a common vertex

Root the trees at the cycle. Let $a$ have at least two child subtrees, each a path, of lengths $\ell_i$ measured in edges from $a$. Write $L=\max_i\ell_i$ and let $E$ be the connected subgraph left after deleting the paths but retaining $a$. The only edges between the deleted vertices and $E$ meet $a$.

**One short path.** Suppose precisely one path has length one and there are at least two longer paths. Color $E$ blue and color only the terminal leaf of a longest path blue on that path. On all the other paths color exactly their first vertices blue. Take $H$ to be the short leaf together with the blue terminal leaf of the longest path. If both are lost, the short leaf and the longest path remain white because $a$ can see both their white first vertices and neither path has a blue vertex. If only the short leaf is lost, the longest path fills inward in $L-1$ rounds and $a$ restores the short leaf in round $L$. If only the terminal leaf is lost, $a$ starts an outward force at once and the longest path fills in $L$ rounds. The other paths complete by round $L-1$ in both cases.

Now lose the initially blue first vertex of a different long path, of length $M\ge2$. The inward front reaches the neighbor of $a$ in round $L-1$. It frees $a$ to start the missing path, which completes at time $L-1+M$. The same reasoning gives finite completion for a loss at the first vertex of any other long path. Every exterior neighbor of $a$ has another neighbor in $E$. For a loss at an exterior neighbor of $a$, that other neighbor restores it in round one. For a loss elsewhere in $E\setminus\{a\}$, a neighbor other than $a$ restores it in round one. The short leaf restores a lost $a$ in round one. Once the missing vertex is restored, the evolution contains the original blue set and completes. This shows that $H$ is minimum and the two one-loss deadlines differ. The path coloring is the one-short path-star construction from the v1.0.1 proof, here with a connected exterior and the stated neighbor condition.

**All paths long.** Suppose each $\ell_i\ge2$. Suppose there is a set $W\subseteq V(E)\setminus(\{a\}\cup N_E(a))$ such that each exterior neighbor of $a$ is adjacent to a member of $W$. Require that the initial blue set $V(E)\setminus W$ fills $E$ in one round and that it still forces $E$ after each of its singleton losses. Color just the first vertex of each path blue and color $V(E)\setminus W$ blue. Use as $H$ the first vertices of two paths, one of length $L$. Losing both leaves them permanently white because $a$ sees two missing first vertices. Losing either member alone lets $a$ restore it in round one. Every force into $W$ in round one has its source outside $a$, and the path loss does not alter those sources' neighborhoods. The restricted one-loss time is exactly $L$.

For an exterior singleton loss, all first vertices remain blue and the exterior evolves exactly as it does in $E$. A loss of $a$ prevents every exterior neighbor from forcing it in round one because that neighbor also sees a white vertex of $W$. Each first vertex of a path sees $a$ and the next vertex white. Thus $a$ is restored no earlier than round two, and the longest path needs another $L-1$ rounds. If a first path vertex is lost instead, it is restored immediately because $W$ misses the neighbors of $a$. This proves minimum size two and a gap between the deadlines.

If $a$ lies off the cycle in a twin-free graph, let $p$ be its parent. Choose $y$ to be the parent of $p$ on the route to the cycle, or a cycle neighbor of $p$ when $p$ is on the cycle. Set $W=\{y\}$. The vertex $y$ has a neighbor other than $p$ in $E$, so it restores in round one even on loss of $a$. The vertex $p$ then restores $a$ in round two. Also, $y$ has no twin in $E$. Any twin other than $a$ would remain a twin in the original graph because neither vertex meets the deleted paths. The vertex $a$ is a leaf of $E$, whereas $y$ has degree at least two there. With two white vertices, a blue vertex can force unless they are twins, since distinct external neighborhoods give a blue vertex seeing exactly one of them. This verifies the forcing condition for all exterior singleton losses. Consequently, an off-cycle vertex with at least two children whose subtrees are paths excludes a twin-free graph whenever the paths are all long or there are at least three paths with precisely one short.

For $a$ on the cycle, label the cycle $0,\ldots,n-1$ with $a=0$. In the following table $w_i$ denotes a nonleaf off-cycle child at site $i$. The existence conditions refer to attachments in $E$.

| Condition | $W$ |
| --- | --- |
| $n\ge7$, or $n\in\{5,6\}$ with an attachment at $2$ or $n-2$ | $\{2,n-2\}$ |
| $n=4$ with an attachment at the opposite vertex | $\{2\}$ |
| $n\ge5$ and $w_{n-1}$ exists, up to reflection | $\{2,w_{n-1}\}$ |
| $w_1$ and $w_{n-1}$ both exist | $\{w_1,w_{n-1}\}$ |

For the first row each white cycle vertex has a near blue source at $1$ or $n-1$ in the initial exterior. If $n\ge7$, losing a ring vertex leaves at least $n-3>\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ blue ring vertices and hence an adjacent pair. The attached trees are blue and permit the usual cycle propagation. For $n=5,6$, an off-cycle neighbor at $2$ or $n-2$ restores that site in round one even on a ring loss. An off-cycle loss, for any length in the first row, can interfere with at most one of the two near sources. Whenever at least one blocker has just filled, any two remaining whites include a nonleaf cycle vertex of a cycle of length at least five. Such a vertex has no twin in a unicyclic graph. Indeed adjacent nonleaf twins must lie on the unique triangle, and nonadjacent nonleaf twins must be the degree-two opposite vertices of the unique square. Every exterior loss in the first row therefore finishes. On loss of $a$ specifically, a blocker is restored in round one from the far side for $n\ge7$ or from the attached tree for $n=5,6$. A near vertex can then force $a$ in round two. If a blocker is still white after round one on a short cycle, the restored blocker on the pentagon or their common neighbor on the hexagon can fill it in round two.

In the second row the opposite vertex has degree at least three in $E$ and hence has no twin by the same triangle and square criterion. It fills initially from either near blue vertex and fills on loss of $a$ from its off-cycle neighbor. In the third row, the sources for the initial colors are $1$ and a child of $w_{n-1}$. An exterior singleton loss can interfere with at most one of these two sources and its neighbors. The remaining pair of whites, if any, includes a blocker that has no twin because it is either a nonleaf off the cycle or a cycle vertex on a cycle of length at least five. On loss of $a$, $w_{n-1}$ is still restored from its child, freeing $n-1$ to restore $a$ in round two. The vertex $2$ fills from $3$ in round one in this loss case. In the fourth row the children of the two blockers restore their respective parents in round one initially. Their branches are disjoint, so a singleton loss leaves at least one of these restorations available and the other blocker is a nonleaf off the cycle with no twin. Both restorations are available on loss of $a$, which fills in round two. This verifies all the exterior conditions in the table and gives slow completion time $L+1$ in every row.


## Extending a certificate by an all-blue tree

**Proposition.** Let a connected graph have a budget-one certificate $(B,H,z)$ with a two-element minimum killing set $H$ and a slow singleton loss $z$. Fix a vertex $x$ such that, under each of the five initial colorings

$$
B,\qquad B\setminus\{h_1\},\qquad B\setminus\{h_2\},\qquad
B\setminus H,\qquad B\setminus\{z\},
$$

where $H=\{h_1,h_2\}$, the vertex $x$ is blue by the end of round one. Attach a new tree at $x$ by a single edge to its first vertex $v$, and suppose $v$ has a neighbor in the new tree. Color the new tree blue and keep $H$ and $z$. The extended graph has a certificate with the same restricted deadline and slow completion time.

For the five colorings, the new vertices stay blue. The only possible additional force into the old graph is from $v$ to $x$ in round one. The condition on $x$ guarantees that the old graph has exactly the same colors at the end of that round. The later old colors agree by induction. Hence losing $H$ still kills, and the two one-loss deadlines stay as before. For every other old singleton loss the projected old process contains the colors of the original process at each round, because the added neighbors are blue. It therefore finishes. If the lost vertex is $v$, its neighbor in the new tree restores it in round one, after which the blue set contains the entire extended initial set. If a deeper vertex of the new tree is lost, $v$ stays blue and the old graph fills. Connectivity then gives a force into the one lost vertex. This proves that the killing set stays minimum.

The proposition also applies to several such attached trees at once if each attachment vertex satisfies the same first-round condition for the old certificate and each new first vertex has a child. This gives unbounded graph families from fixed certificates. The census replay finds a smaller graph from a previously excluded family by cutting off one branch with at least two vertices. It finds a certificate on that smaller graph satisfying the first-round condition at the cut, then replays the lifted certificate on the original graph. The replay processes smaller graphs first, so a graph already excluded by this reduction can serve as the smaller graph for another cut.


**Single-leaf extension.** The attached tree may consist of just one leaf at $x$ if the old graph fills from $B$ even with all forces from $x$ suppressed. The first-round condition still guarantees agreement for the five displayed colorings. When the new leaf itself is lost, the suppressed process fills the old graph by monotonicity, after which $x$ can restore the leaf. In fact this extra condition characterizes whether that new singleton loss is survivable. While the leaf stays white, $x$ cannot force any old neighbor, so the old colors follow the suppressed process. If $x$ restores the leaf, it already has no white old neighbor. Suppressing its forces from then on cannot alter the old evolution because blue vertices remain blue and $x$ has no remaining old target. Thus full completion requires that the old suppressed process fill the old graph.

## Triangles with one path per site

**Theorem.** Attach at most one path at each vertex of a triangle. If some path has at least two edges, the graph fails EveryMinimumSupport. The shallow triangles are classified above.

The sole-site theorem handles one attached path. Otherwise write the three lengths in decreasing order as $L\ge p\ge q\ge0$ with $p>0$, and name the sites $a,b,c$ in that order. A missing path has length zero. For $L=2,3$, the 14 triples are given by $1\le p\le L$ and $0\le q\le p$. Each has an explicit finite certificate in the table SMALL_WITNESSES in verify_triangle_paths.py. That table labels $a,b,c$ as $0,1,2$, then labels the vertices of each attached path outward in site order. The replay checks the killing pair, every possible singleton loss, and the two exact one-loss times for each row.

Let $L\ge4$, and first suppose $(p,q)\ne(1,1)$. Color the triangle blue and color only the first vertex of the longest path blue on that path. On each other path color the odd positions blue up to but excluding the terminal leaf, measuring positions outwards from its site. In particular a short leaf starts white. Take $H=\{b,c\}$. With both sites lost, the two white sites block $a$. At a lost site, an existing blue first vertex sees both the site and its successor white, all blue vertices further along the path have two white neighbors, and the terminal leaf is white. Thus neither site becomes blue and the killing pair kills.

If either member alone is lost, $a$ restores the site in round one and the longest path completes in $L-1$ rounds. Each of the other paths, once its site is blue, takes at most $\lceil L/2\rceil$ rounds from its initial path colors. The white vertices along it are filled successively from the site, passing each isolated blue without a separate force. Since $1+\lceil L/2\rceil\le L-1$, the restricted time is $L-1$. If the first vertex of the longest path is lost instead, $a$ restores it in round one and that path takes $L$ rounds. A loss at another blue path vertex still allows that entire path to fill from its site with the full triangle blue. If $a$ is lost, at least one of $b,c$ has no short white leaf and can restore $a$ in round one. This covers all singleton losses.

For $(p,q)=(1,1)$ let $u$ be the leaf at $b$ and $v$ the leaf at $c$. Start with $a,c,u$ and the first vertex of the longest path blue. Take $H=\{c,u\}$. With both lost, $a$ sees two white sites, which stay white, and neither leaf can restore them. On loss of $u$, $a$ restores $b$ in round one and both short leaves recover in round two. On loss of $c$, $u$ restores $b$ in round one, $a$ restores $c$ in round two, and $c$ restores $v$ in round three. The longest path takes $L-1$ rounds in both cases, giving restricted time $\max(L-1,3)$. Losing the first vertex of the longest path blocks $a$ while $b$ is white. The site $c$ sees $b$ and $v$ white while $u$ restores $b$ in round one. The missing first vertex is then restored in round two and the path completes at time $L+1$. The other singleton loss, at $a$, first restores $b$ from $u$, then restores $a$ from $b$, and fills the remaining paths. Hence every singleton loss completes and $H$ is minimum.



## Extra rigidity on a triangle with two attachment sites

Let $a,b$ be the attachment sites of a triangle, let $c$ be bare, and suppose $b$ has exactly one pendant path of length $L$. The attachment tree at $a$ is initially unrestricted.

**A longer path at the other site.** Suppose some vertex $u$ of the attachment tree at $a$, including $a$ itself, has a descending pendant path of length $K\ge L+2$. Color all vertices blue except the whole path at $b$ and all but the first vertex of this selected path at $u$. Use $H=\{b,c\}$. If both sites in $H$ are lost, neither is restored. Their only possible blue ring source $a$ sees both white, and the path at $b$ has no seed. If exactly one is lost, $a$ restores it in round one. The path at $b$ takes another $L$ rounds, and the selected path at $u$ has an uninterrupted outward front of length $K-1$. Removing $u$ instead delays that front by one round. The bare $c$ restores a lost $a$ in round one. An off-cycle $u$ is restored in round one from its parent toward $a$, whose other neighbors are blue. The slow time is therefore $K$, whereas the restricted time is $\max(L+1,K-1)$. The loss of the selected first vertex is immediately restored by $u$. For every other off-cycle singleton loss, the first vertices and their centers stay available, and once the two paths fill the remaining lost vertex is restored. A loss at $a$ when $u$ is off-cycle is restored at once from $c$. Thus every singleton loss completes.

**Two short enough paths at the other site.** If some vertex $u$ in the attachment at $a$ has two descending pendant paths whose lengths are at most $2L-1$, start with the whole triangle blue, the whole path at $b$ white and all other vertices blue except for the interiors of the two selected paths at $u$. On those paths color position one and the even positions strictly before the leaf blue, as in the compressed pair construction. Use the two first vertices at $u$ as $H$. Losing both kills. Losing one restores it in round one and gives time at most $\lceil(2L)/2\rceil=L$ on the two paths. The path at $b$ starts immediately in either comparison, so the restricted time is $L$. If $a$ is lost, $c$ restores it in round one and the all-white path at $b$ must wait one round before it starts. It then finishes in round $L+1$. If $u=a$, the paths at $u$ finish by round $L$ since their first vertices survived and there are at most $L-1$ white vertices on each path. Other singleton losses on the triangle are restored from a ring neighbor in round one, with the first vertices at $u$ still blue. If $u$ is off the cycle and is lost, its parent can restore it because every other retained neighbor of that parent is blue. Otherwise any off-cycle singleton loss outside the three selected paths can be restored once those paths fill. Loss of an internal blue vertex on one of the two selected paths does not prevent the outward front from its blue center. This proves minimum size two and the strict gap.

These arguments exclude every such triangle with $L\ge2$. If the tree at $a$ is a single path, use the classification with one path per site. Otherwise choose a deepest branching vertex of that tree, allowing the root $a$. Its child subtrees are paths. If the maximum of their lengths is at least $L+2$, use the first argument. If the maximum is at most $L+1$, use the second, since $L+1\le 2L-1$ for $L\ge2$.

**Some final configurations with $L=1$.** The longer-path argument already applies if any descending pendant path on the $a$ side has length at least three. For two pendant paths at the root $a$ of lengths $1,2$, color all blue except the first vertex of the long path and the leaf at $b$. Take $H$ to consist of the short leaf and the long terminal leaf at $a$. Losing both kills. Losing the short leaf restores the first vertex of the long path from its terminal leaf in round one and the short leaf in round two. Losing the terminal restores it in round two from the outward force. Losing $c$ instead starts with both $a$ and $b$ blocked by a white off-cycle neighbor. The long terminal leaf restores its first vertex in round one, $a$ restores $c$ in round two and $b$ restores its leaf in round three. The loss of $a$ or $b$ is restored by $c$.

If both selected paths at $a$ have length two and it has no direct leaf, leave the second path and $b$ white and every other vertex blue. Take both vertices of the first path as $H$. If they are both lost, $a$ is permanently blocked by its two white first vertices. Restricted singleton losses restore the first path and $b$ by round one, then fill the second path in two more rounds. If $c$ is lost, the leaf at $b$ restores $b$ in round one. Then $b$ restores $c$ in round two because $a$ is blue, and the white second path takes two further rounds. The loss of $a$ is restored from the first path and the loss at the leaf of $b$ is rescued by $c$ restoring $b$ in round one. For both of these root constructions all additional branches at $a$ are blue and have nonleaf first vertices. Any loss of such a first vertex is restored by its child in round one. A deeper singleton loss leaves the required ring forces intact, so it is restored after the displayed white vertices fill. The possible direct-leaf twin case is covered by the twin theorem.

Finally, suppose $a$ leads down a stem of length $d\ge1$ to a vertex $u$ with a short leaf $s$ and a path of length two with first vertex $h$. The only other branches along the stem, if present, are pendant paths of length two. Start with the entire stem from $a$ through $u$ white. Color blue $b,c,s,h$ and just the first vertex on each side path. Use $H=\{s,h\}$. Both losses make the two arms at $u$ permanently white. If $s$ alone is lost, $c$ restores $a$ in round one and the stem front reaches $u$ in round $d+1$. The missing leaf and the terminal of the long path fill in round $d+2$. If $h$ is lost, $s$ restores $u$ in round one, but $u$ sees its stem neighbor and its missing first vertex white until the front from $a$ has reached that neighbor in round $d$. It restores $h$ in round $d+1$ and the terminal in round $d+2$. Side paths fill one round after their stem vertices become blue.

On loss of $b$ instead, the bare $c$ sees both $a,b$ white. The short leaf at $u$ restores $u$ in round one, and the stem runs inward from $u$ to $a$, which becomes blue in round $d+1$. The site $b$ is restored in round $d+2$ and its leaf in round $d+3$. Loss of $c$ also permits the reverse stem front, after which $a$ restores $c$ and $b$ restores its leaf. If the first vertex of a side path is lost, both fronts along the stem are present. Even if the affected stem vertex is initially blocked from making a force toward the other end, the two fronts fill its two stem neighbors, after which it can restore the lost first vertex. All singleton losses therefore finish.

## Time comparisons across distinct pendant centers
The following constructions apply in any finite connected graph with the specified pendant paths. After deleting their vertices other than their centers, the retained graph is connected. A center can be on the cycle or in an attached tree. Unselected branches and all other vertices of the retained graph are initially blue, except for one center in the equal-length construction.

**Two pairs of paths.** Let $u,v$ be distinct centers with two pendant paths each, of lengths $A\ge B\ge1$ at $u$ and $C\ge D\ge1$ at $v$. Suppose the four paths outside their centers are disjoint. Color only the terminal leaves on the two paths at $u$ and only the first vertices on the two paths at $v$. Let $H$ consist of those first vertices at $v$. Losing them both leaves both paths permanently white because $v$ sees two white neighbors along them. Losing either one lets $v$ restore it in round one. The worst restricted completion time at $v$ is $C$. At $u$ the paths fill inward until the shorter is filled in $B-1$ rounds. The longer path then has $A-B$ white vertices between two advancing fronts, so its deadline is $B-1+\lceil(A-B)/2\rceil$. The restricted deadline is the maximum of these two deadlines.

Removing the longer path's leaf at $u$ makes that entire path white. The shorter path must fill in $B-1$ rounds before $u$ can begin the missing path. The completion time at $u$ is exactly $A+B-1$. Thus there is a gap whenever $A+B-1>C$.

Every singleton loss completes. The path colors permit the stated recovery for either terminal loss at $u$ and either first-vertex loss at $v$. If $u$ is lost, a terminal front can reach it from its own path. If another retained vertex is lost, the paths at $u$ can still fill from their leaves, with $u$ blue throughout. Once the two paths at $u$ are blue, the retained graph has just the lost vertex white, and a retained neighbor can restore it. Its initially blue neighbors on the selected paths at $v$ add no further white neighbor to any retained vertex. The paths at $v$ fill once its center is blue. This verifies minimum cardinality two without any acyclicity requirement on the retained graph.

When both lengths at each center are $(L,1)$ with $L\ge2$ and $u,v$ are nonadjacent, use a different coloring. Start with $u$ white, the rest of the retained graph blue, and just the first vertex blue on all four paths. Keep $H$ at $v$. The short leaf restores $u$ in round one, and with either loss in $H$ the other path at $v$ still has its first vertex blue. Since $u$ is not adjacent to $v$, the missing first vertex is restored in round one. The worst restricted completion time is $L$. Removing the first vertex of the long path at $u$ instead gives a wholly white path that cannot start before $u$ has turned blue in round one, so it finishes in round $L+1$. If the short leaf at $u$ is lost, a retained neighbor other than $v$ restores $u$ in round one and $u$ then restores its leaf. If another retained vertex is lost, $u$ is restored by its short leaf before the sole remaining white retained vertex is restored. All path gates are blue in this last case, so they do not obstruct the latter restoration. Losing both vertices of $H$ still kills.

In a twin-free graph these arguments cover all nonadjacent centers with two pendant paths each. If the largest lengths differ, put the larger at $u$, which gives $A+B-1\ge A>C$. If they agree and at least one shorter length is at least two, put that pair at $u$. The only remaining lengths are $(L,1)$ on both sides, since $(1,1)$ creates twin leaves. In particular, in a unicyclic graph, any two off-cycle branching vertices whose child subtrees are paths are nonadjacent, and the two-center result applies. The two constructions adapt the strict and equal nonadjacent clock arguments from v1.0.1, here using only connectivity of the retained graph.

**A pair of paths and one remote path.** Suppose instead $u$ has two pendant paths of lengths $A,B$ and $v\ne u$ has a pendant path of length $L$. The three paths outside their centers are disjoint and avoid both centers. Assume $\max(A,B)\le 2L-3$. Color the retained graph blue. At $v$ only the first vertex of its selected path is blue. At $u$ on each selected path, color position one and every even position strictly before the terminal vertex blue. Take $H$ to be the two first vertices at $u$.

After loss of both, every remaining blue vertex on the paths at $u$ has two white neighbors, and $u$ sees two white first vertices. These paths stay incomplete. Losing either member alone lets $u$ restore it in round one. A path of length $m$ with a restored first vertex then fills in $\lceil(m-1)/2\rceil$ additional rounds. Its white vertices fill successively from the center end, with each even blue vertex becoming ready as soon as its predecessor is filled. Thus the restricted deadline is

$$
\max\bigl(L-1,\,\lceil(\max(A,B)+1)/2\rceil\bigr)=L-1.
$$

If $v$ is lost, the first vertex on its path cannot advance until $v$ is restored. A retained neighbor restores $v$ in round one because all other retained vertices and all selected first vertices are blue. The remote path therefore takes exactly $L$ rounds. A loss at $u$ is restored by the same retained-neighbor argument. A loss at an initially blue internal vertex on a path at $u$ still permits completion from its first vertex once the center is blue. A loss of the first vertex at $v$ is restored from $v$. If any other retained vertex is lost, all three paths fill with their blue first vertices and centers, and the remaining lost vertex is then restored. The resulting certificate covers adjacent as well as nonadjacent centers. In particular, it includes equal pairs $(L,1)$ at adjacent centers for every $L\ge3$, even though the construction uses only one of the two paths at the remote center.



## Adjacent short pairs and exhaustion of two sites on longer cycles

Suppose two adjacent centers $u,v$ have pendant paths of lengths one and two each. Let $s$ be the short leaf at $u$, and let $d$ be the terminal of the length-two path at $v$. Require that $s$ is the only leaf adjacent to $u$, and that $v$ has a neighbor other than $u$ in the retained graph after deleting the four selected paths. Color all blue except $v,s,d$. Choose $H$ to consist of both vertices of the length-two path at $u$. Losing both blocks $u$ from filling either of its two white first vertices. With one lost, the other restores it in round one, as the path either starts from its blue terminal or its blue first vertex and blue $u$. The short leaf at $v$ restores $v$ in round one, and $s,d$ fill in round two. If the first vertex on the length-two path at $v$ is lost instead, $v$ is still restored in round one, after which its path needs two additional rounds. Thus the slow time is three.

Losing $u$ lets the fully blue length-two path at $u$ restore $u$ in round one. If the short leaf at $v$ is lost, a retained neighbor other than $u$ restores $v$ in round one. For a singleton loss elsewhere in the retained graph, both centers are blue by round one. The lost vertex has a neighbor in the retained graph other than $u$, unless it is itself a leaf at $u$. The sole-leaf condition excludes the latter possibility. The retained neighbor can restore it because the other retained vertices are blue and the only remaining white neighbors outside the retained graph are on the selected paths at $u,v$. At $v$ the first vertices of both selected paths are blue for this loss, so it can act as a restoring neighbor as well. Once the retained colors have recovered, $s,d$ fill. This proves the certificate in any connected graph with the stated paths and retained conditions.

In particular any two adjacent cycle sites with the pairs $(2,1)$ and $(2,1)$ give this certificate in a twin-free graph. More generally, if two distinct attachment trees are not single paths, take a deepest branching vertex in each, including the cycle site if needed. Each has at least two descending pendant paths and the four selected paths are disjoint. Nonadjacent centers are covered by the two-center comparisons. For adjacent centers, unequal largest lengths or a shorter length at least two give the strict comparison. Equal pairs $(L,1)$ with $L\ge3$ are covered by the compressed remote argument. The only remaining case is the adjacent short pair just proved. Thus a twin-free graph with two nonpath attachment trees at distinct sites fails, even if it has further sites.

Now consider a cycle of length $n\ge4$ with exactly two attachment sites. If the graph has twins or both rooted attachments are nonpaths, use the preceding classifications. Otherwise at least one rooted attachment is a single path. The two-site path constructions apply to cyclic distance one or two and to distances for which the two arcs differ by at most one. For distance at least three, apply the earlier separated-site argument with the two sites as the independent set. When $n$ is odd the initial white count is $n-4$, which is odd. When $n$ is even and the longer arc has length at least five, add an isolated blue at distance three along the longer arc from the site whose two neighbors form $H$. That vertex is separated from the closest initial blue vertices by at least one white vertex on each side. The resulting white count is $n-5$, again odd. The only even cycles of distance at least three with longer arc less than five are the opposite placements on $C_6$ and $C_8$, already covered by the two-site path result. This proves failure for every longer cycle with exactly two attachment sites.


## Completion of the two-site triangle classification

The remaining case in the preceding triangle analysis has a direct leaf at $b$ and a nonpath attachment at $a$. We first give three constructions at a branching vertex $u$ off the cycle. Its child paths consist exactly of a short leaf $s$ and a path of length $K\ge2$ with first vertex $h$. The path from an ancestor $p$ to $u$ is called the stem in the following constructions. Its length is $m$.

**A leaf at the upper end of the stem.** Suppose $p$ has a leaf $e$ and every extra branch at an internal stem vertex is a pendant path of length two. Color all vertices blue except the internal stem vertices, the tail after $h$, and the terminal of each internal side path. If $m=1$, leave $u$ white as well. Use $H=\{e,s\}$ and slow loss $h$. When $m\ge2$, losing both leaves blocks each end of the stem and neither side-path gate can force its white stem parent. When $m=1$, after both losses $p$ sees $e,u$ white and $h$ sees $u$ and its successor white, so $u$ is never restored.

For $m\ge2$, a singleton loss in $H$ still allows the opposite boundary to start a front. The $m-1$ internal vertices fill and the lost leaf is restored in round $m$. Side paths need one round after their stem parents, and the long path at $u$ finishes in round $K-1$. If $h$ is lost, $u$ is initially blocked by its white stem neighbor and cannot restore $h$ until round $m$. The long path finishes at $m+K-1>\max(m,K-1)$. If $m=1$, on a restricted loss $u$ is restored in round one from $s$ or from $p$, and all remaining vertices fill by round $K$. On loss of $h$, the blue $s$ restores $u$ in round one, then $u$ restores $h$ in round two, giving time $K+1$.

Other singleton losses leave both leaves of $H$ available and allow the stem to fill. If an internal side-path gate is lost, the two stem fronts approach its parent from both ends, filling the neighbors of the possibly blocked parent, which then restores the missing gate. If an outside vertex initially blocks the front from $p$, the surviving front from $u$ completes the stem, and the outside loss is then restored. For $m=1$, $s$ restores $u$ independently if the outside loss prevents a force. Loss of either initially blue center is restored from its own leaf. Thus the pair is minimum.

**A bare stem with a leaf-free upper end.** Suppose $K=2$, $m\ge2$, every internal stem vertex has degree two, and $p$ has no leaf neighbor. There is a neighbor of $p$ outside the stem, on the route to the cycle or on the cycle itself. Start with all blue except the internal stem vertices and $s$. Use both vertices of the length-two path at $u$ as $H$. Losing both keeps the two child paths at $u$ white. Losing one restores it in round one through the other vertex of that path. The front from $p$ colors all internal stem vertices by round $m-1$, after which $u$ restores its leaf in round $m$. If $p$ is lost, an exterior neighbor restores it in round one. The front waits one round because $u$ is blocked by $s$ and its white stem neighbor. Its leaf is restored in round $m+1$. If $u$ is lost, its intact length-two path restores it in round one. An outside vertex lost at a neighbor of $p$ has another neighbor, since $p$ has no leaf neighbors. That neighbor lies outside the white stem and can restore the loss in round one. Any other loss outside the stem leaves the front available, so the stem finishes and the loss is then restored.

**Adjacent upper end with a length-two side path.** Suppose $K=2$, $m=1$, and the off-cycle parent $p$ of $u$ has a separate pendant path of length two. Let $y$ be the parent of $p$. Suppose $y$ has no leaf neighbor. Color all blue except $p$, the leaf $s$, and the terminal of the selected path at $p$. Again use both vertices of the length-two path at $u$ as $H$. Losing both kills, whereas losing one restores it in round one. The blue $y$ restores $p$ in round one, so $s$ and the white terminal fill in round two. For the slow loss remove the first vertex of the selected path at $p$. The vertex $p$ is restored in round one as before, then it restores the missing first vertex in round two and the terminal in round three.

The two initially blue vertices on the path at $u$ restore a lost $u$ in round one. A lost $y$ is restored from further toward or along the cycle in round one, so it can then restore $p$. If a different outside loss $x$ obstructs the force from $y$, the vertex $x$ has a neighbor other than $y$ because $y$ has no leaf neighbor. Such a neighbor can restore $x$ independently of the white $p$, as the edge $yp$ is toward an off-cycle subtree. After $p$ is restored the selected paths and other losses finish. These arguments give minimum size two in this last case as well.

**Exhaustion.** In the residual twin-free triangle, no pendant path on the $a$ side can have length at least three, by the earlier comparison to the leaf at $b$. If two branching vertices have all their children on pendant paths, they are incomparable in the rooted tree and nonadjacent. The two-center obstruction then applies. Otherwise there is a unique such branching vertex $u$, and all branching vertices in the attachment at $a$ are on its ancestor chain. Every subtree off that chain is a path of length one or two.

If $u=a$, the cycle fork constructions apply. If $u\ne a$, the common-center constructions exclude two or more all-long branches and exclude one short branch with two or more long branches. Thus $u$ has exactly the short leaf and a length-two path. If it has no branching ancestor, use the stem construction from the preceding triangle analysis. Otherwise choose its nearest branching ancestor $p$, with no extra branches strictly between $p$ and $u$. If $p$ has a leaf, use the two-leaf stem construction. If $p$ has no leaf and $m\ge2$, use the bare stem construction. When $m=1$ and $p=a$, all its side paths are of length two, so the preceding triangle stem construction applies. When $m=1$ and $p$ is off the cycle, use the adjacent construction if its parent $y$ has no leaf neighbor. If $y$ has one, start the two-leaf stem at $y$ instead. Its sole internal stem vertex $p$ has only length-two side paths. This exhausts the remaining two-site triangles.

Together with the two-site result for longer cycles, the only connected unicyclic graph with exactly two attachment sites satisfying EveryMinimumSupport is a triangle with singleton leaves at those sites, or a triangle with direct leaves only and at least two at each of its two sites.


## Complete classification on a triangular core

For three attachment sites, the established results handle twins, two nonpath attachments, and three single-path attachments. It remains to consider a twin-free graph with one nonpath attachment at $a$ and two single-path sites $b,c$. Let $M$ be the longer of the lengths at $b,c$.

If there is a branching vertex $u$ in the attachment at $a$ with two pendant paths of lengths at most $2M-1$, apply the earlier short-path comparison with the length-$M$ site as $b$. The third site $c$ and its entire attachment are blue initially. In the original construction no compared loss is at $c$ and it remains blue, so the two times agree. If $c$ itself is lost, the ring site $a$ can restore it because every first path vertex at $a$ is blue in that loss case. If a vertex in the attachment at $c$ is lost, all ring vertices are blue and its adjacent blue neighbor within that attachment or at $c$ can restore it immediately.

If the attachment at $a$ has a descending pendant path of length $K>1+\lceil M/2\rceil$ at a vertex $u$, use $H=\{b,c\}$. Color all three ring vertices blue and on the selected path at $u$ color just its first vertex blue. On the paths at $b,c$ use the odd positions strictly before the terminal as blue seeds. Thus a path of length one starts white. All remaining vertices at $a$ are blue. With both members of $H$ lost, $a$ sees two white cycle neighbors and the paths at $b,c$ do not restore them. With one member lost, $a$ restores it in round one. The affected paths at $b,c$ fill by $1+\lceil M/2\rceil$, and the path at $u$ takes $K-1$ rounds. For the slow loss remove $u$. Its retained parent restores it in round one when $u$ is off the cycle. When $u=a$, an unselected branch of the nonpath attachment restores $a$ in round one. Consequently the selected path waits a round, with finish time $K$. Other singleton losses fill because the rings stay blue or the lost site is restored by its own fully blue branch, and then any lost path seed can be restored from its blue site or the remaining blue front. In particular, on loss of $a$ with $u\ne a$, the first vertex of the branch leading to $u$ has no white neighbor besides $a$, since the selected tail begins at depth at least three. All cases have minimum killing cardinality two.

If $M\ge2$, take a deepest branching vertex of the attachment at $a$. If its longest path has length $K\le2M-1$, the first comparison applies. Otherwise $K\ge2M>1+\lceil M/2\rceil$, and the second applies. If $M=1$, the second comparison already handles any pendant path of length at least three on the $a$ side. For the remaining $M=1$ graphs, two incomparable branching vertices with pendant paths give the two-center obstruction. A unique such vertex off the cycle has exactly a short leaf and a length-two path, after the common-center results. Its ancestor chain is handled by the three stem cases of the two-site exhaustion unless it has no branching ancestor, or its nearest branching ancestor is the root with only length-two side branches and is adjacent to it. In those exceptional stem cases the earlier triangle stem witness still works with the leaf at $c$ blue. The ring uses $c$ as a source under restricted losses and $c$ is blue under the slow loss at $b$. If the new leaf at $c$ is lost, neither front at the triangle may start early, but the reverse stem front from the intact gates reaches $a$, and both leaves on the triangle recover. A loss of $c$ itself is also survivable. The other losses and the deadlines remain as proved for that stem coloring.

We finish with the cases where all branches at $a$ are paths of length at most two. For lengths one and two at $a$, use the short-long cycle fork palette of the two-site result, leaving the two leaves at $b,c$ white and choosing $b$ as the slow loss. The same two seed leaves at $a$ form $H$, and each restricted loss fills by round two. When $b$ is lost, $a$ is blocked by the missing long head and $c$ is blocked by its own leaf. The long terminal restores its head in round one, $a$ restores $b$ in round two, and the two cycle leaves complete in round three. The other ring loss at $c$ is symmetric, and loss of $a$ is restored by its short leaf.

When all chosen branches at $a$ have length two, leave one entire such path, $a$ itself, and the leaf at $c$ white. The leaf at $b$ and all other vertices begin blue. Take $H$ to be $b$ and its leaf. A separate length-two branch colors $a$ in round one. After both losses, the white $b$ is still blocked from both sources because $a$ has its white path and $c$ has its white leaf. With only one loss in $H$, the leaf restores a lost $b$ in round one, or $b$ stays blue, and the white path at $a$ fills by round three. If $c$ is lost, $b$ cannot restore it while $a$ is also white. Once $a$ is restored in round one, $b$ restores $c$ in round two, and the white path at $a$ finishes in round four. A loss in the separate branch at $a$ leaves the blue pair $b,c$, with the blue leaf at $b$, available to restore $a$ in round one. Other singleton losses in extra branches at $a$ are likewise recoverable. Both root constructions permit extra fully blue nonleaf branches at $a$.

The positive members among all unicyclic graphs with a triangular core are therefore the pure triangle and the direct-leaf cases with counts $(1,0,0)$, $(1,1,0)$, or every nonzero count at least two. Every triangular core with a deep attachment fails.

## A long bare interval between two pendant paths
Let sites $a,b$ have a cycle arc $0,1,\ldots,D$ with $a=0$ and $b=D\ge5$, with every interior vertex bare. The complementary arc has at least two internal vertices. Suppose each site has a selected pendant path and neither site has an unselected direct leaf. Orient the arc so that the selected lengths satisfy $L_a\le L_b$. Other attachments are arbitrary. Color the two selected paths entirely white, color just $1,2,3$ blue on the displayed arc, and color the rest of the graph blue. Take $H=\{1,3\}$ and $z=2$.

On these three singleton losses and the double loss, the two sites become blue in round one from their neighbors on the complementary arc. Each site sees a white selected gate and its ring neighbor on the displayed arc, so it cannot begin a force into that arc until that ring neighbor is blue. When both $1,3$ are lost, the remaining seed $2$ is isolated in the arc; both sites are blocked and the process stalls. Losing $1$ alone leaves a left front that restores $1$ in round one and a right front from $3$ that reaches $D-1$ in round $D-4$. Losing $3$ alone has the right front advance from $2$ and reach $D-1$ in round $D-3$. In both cases the selected path at $a$ starts after round one. Its finish time $1+L_a$ is at most $D-3+L_b$, the restricted time. Losing $2$ leaves $1,3$ unable to advance in round one. After $a$ becomes blue, $1$ restores $2$ in round two. Vertex $3$ then advances right and reaches $D-1$ in round $D-2$. The path at $b$ finishes in round $D-2+L_b$.

For other singleton losses, the entire run $1,2,3$ is blue and both its fronts fill the displayed arc, including the two sites, irrespective of any missing force from the complementary arc. If an internal vertex of the complementary arc was lost, another internal vertex adjoining it can restore it after the displayed arc is blue, since neither selected path meets that source. A loss in an unselected branch at a site cannot permanently block a selected path. If its first vertex was lost it has an initially blue child to restore it, and deeper losses do not obstruct the selected path. Losses at other off-cycle vertices leave the two arc fronts available and are restored once the cycle is blue and the selected paths have filled. Hence all singleton losses finish and the two deadlines differ.


## A local killing pair with a remote long path

Suppose a cycle site $a$ has a single pendant path of length $M\ge5$. Choose two other sites $p,q$ whose attachments are single paths of lengths at most $M$. Require that no bare cycle vertex has $a,q$ as its two neighbors. One of the following conditions on the arc from $p$ to $q$ suffices.

1. The arc has one, two or three internal vertices, all bare.
2. It has one internal vertex $x$ whose attachment is a single path of length at most $M$.

The site $a$ lies on the complementary arc. All attachments outside the displayed sites can be arbitrary. We give a budget-one certificate in which the whole path at $a$ is initially white. Unless noted, all other vertices begin blue. At $p$, use the first vertex and even positions strictly before the terminal as blue seeds. At $q$, use the even positions strictly before the terminal, so its first vertex starts white. Once a site has both cycle neighbors and its first path vertex blue, the resulting outward front fills each remaining white path vertex in one round. If the first vertex has to be filled as well, the path of length $J$ needs at most $\lceil(J+1)/2\rceil$ rounds including that first force. With the first vertex removed, the remaining even seeds are isolated among whites and cannot restore it without that force. This also holds when $J=1$.

For the first condition, include the first vertex of the path at $p$ and the last bare vertex of the arc in $H$. If the gap has only one vertex it is initially blue. If the gap has two or three vertices, leave its first vertex white and color the other vertices blue. When both members are lost, $p$ and $q$ are blocked by a white ring neighbor and a white path gate. Any interior blue vertex left in the gap is isolated. When the first member alone is lost, the surviving ring seed restores the first gap vertex in at most one round. When the ring seed alone is lost, $p$ starts the front immediately. The gap is full by round two. The two selected side paths therefore finish by $2+\lceil(M+1)/2\rceil\le M$.

For the second condition, when the middle path has length one, color $x$ white and its leaf blue, with that leaf as the second member of $H$. When the middle path has length at least two, color $x$ blue and color positions of odd index strictly before its terminal blue on its path. Set $x$ as the second member of $H$ in that case. With both members lost, $p,q$ have white first path vertices and see white $x$. In the middle path the short leaf is missing, or the isolated odd seeds cannot restore their white parent. Thus the arc stalls. With just one loss in $H$, the intact short leaf restores $x$ or the blue gate at $p$ enables $p$ to restore $x$ in round one whenever $x$ is white. The selected paths at $p,q$ finish by $1+\lceil(M+1)/2\rceil$. The path at $x$ needs at most $1+\lceil M/2\rceil$ rounds. These bounds are at most $M$. In both conditions the initially blue neighbors at $a$ are unaffected by either restricted loss and its white path takes exactly $M$ rounds.

For an arbitrary singleton loss, the internal arc can still be completed. In the case of a bare gap, if $p$ is lost the surviving seed at the $q$ end fills the gap and restores $p$. If $q$ is lost, the intact gate at $p$ lets its front fill the gap and restore $q$. If there is a middle vertex $x$ with a short leaf, the intact leaf restores $x$ whenever the loss is elsewhere. With the longer middle path, $x$ is blue and has its first path vertex blue whenever the loss is outside the selected pair or that path. In either case $x$ can restore a lost $p$ or $q$. When the loss is elsewhere on the ring, the bare gap or middle vertex fills from its surviving seed. Among the arc boundaries and the exterior, only the blue vertices $a,q$ may still be blocked by their white path gates. An outside lost site with a fully blue attachment is restored by that attachment. An outside lost bare vertex has a ring neighbor other than the two blocked sites by hypothesis, and it is restored from that neighbor. A lost $a$ also has a neighbor other than $q$, from which it can be restored. With an off-cycle singleton loss, the ring completes from its intact sources and the selected paths subsequently fill, including the lost vertex if it belongs to one of them. Once the remaining ring is blue, an off-cycle loss in an entirely blue attachment can be restored as well. This proves the minimum killing cardinality. To obtain a slow loss, remove either ring neighbor of $a$. The path at $a$ cannot start in round one, so completion takes at least $M+1$ rounds.

**Corollary.** Every twin-free graph with at least seven sites, at most one nonpath attachment, and a site with a single pendant path of length at least five fails EveryMinimumSupport. Choose a site $a$ of maximum length among the single-path sites. In the cyclic list of sites, delete $a$ and the possible nonpath site. There is a run of three consecutive remaining sites in the original list. If the nonpath site exists, the surviving sites form at most two runs and at least five survive. A run of three can be chosen with at most one member nearest to $a$ in either cyclic direction, since one end of the run of surviving sites faces the nonpath site. If there is no nonpath site, choose three from the interior of the single run, which has at least six members. A site that is not nearest to $a$ in either cyclic direction cannot share a bare neighbor with $a$.

If a positive bare gap occurs between two of the three chosen sites, orient the pair so that $q$ is an endpoint that is not nearest to $a$. For one through three bare vertices, use the first local condition. For four or more, use the long bare interval theorem, since the other arc has at least two internal vertices. If there is no bare gap between the three chosen sites, they are consecutive ring vertices and the second local condition applies with a qualifying $q$ at an end. The previously proved exclusion for two nonpath attachment trees extends the corollary to arbitrary remaining attachments in a twin-free graph of this site count, whenever at least one single-path site has length at least five.


## Staggered recovery on a short cycle interval

Consider four consecutive cycle vertices $p,x,q,t$ on a cycle of length at least five. Suppose $p,x,q$ have single pendant paths, and $t$ has no direct leaf. Write their path lengths at $p,x,q$ as $P,X,Q$. At $p$ initially color the first vertex and the even positions strictly before the terminal blue. Leave the paths at $x,q$ entirely white and leave $q$ itself white. The rest of the graph is blue. Use as $H$ the first path vertex at $p$ and the cycle vertex $x$. Assume $\lceil(P+1)/2\rceil\le1+\max(X,Q)$.

If both members of $H$ are lost, $t$ restores $q$ in round one, but $p,q$ both see their white path gates and white $x$. Neither can restore $x$, and the path of $x$ is entirely white. If either member alone is lost, $q$ is restored in round one from $t$, and a missing $x$ is restored in round one from $p$. The two paths at $x,q$ finish in rounds $1+X$ and $1+Q$. The path at $p$ finishes within $\lceil(P+1)/2\rceil$ rounds because its gate either stays blue or is restored in round one. The restricted time is $1+\max(X,Q)$.

If $t$ is lost, its other ring neighbor restores it in round one, while $x$ is blocked by white $q$ and its own path. Then $t$ restores $q$ in round two, and the paths at $x,q$ start one round later than in the restricted case. The slow time is $2+\max(X,Q)$. In the remaining singleton losses, a lost $p$ is restored by the other ring neighbor of $p$, and $q$ is still restored from $t$. These sources are available because the cycle has at least five vertices. If the other ring neighbor of $t$ is lost, the next vertex on that side can restore it independently of $q$. This next vertex may be $p$, whose gate and neighbor $x$ are blue at that point. If a first off-cycle neighbor at $t$ is lost, it has a blue child to restore it. When a different retained vertex is lost, the required forces into $x,q$ and the selected paths can proceed, and the solitary remaining loss is restored. An off-cycle loss at an internal blue position on the path at $p$ can be filled by its outward front. Every singleton loss therefore finishes.

A related coloring works on five consecutive vertices $p,x,q,y,t$ when $x,y$ are bare, $p,q$ have single paths and $t$ has no direct leaf. Use the same seeds and $H$ at $p$, keep $q$ blue, leave $y$ and the entire path at $q$ white, and otherwise color all blue. Both losses in $H$ leave the white $x$ permanently blocked between $p,q$. A single restricted loss restores the missing gate or $x$ in round one. The vertex $t$ restores $y$ in round one, so the path at $q$ completes in round $1+Q$, which dominates the time at $p$ if $\lceil(P+1)/2\rceil\le1+Q$. Loss of $t$ postpones the restoration of $y$ to round two and yields slow time $2+Q$. For a loss of either $p$ or $q$, the bare blue $x$ restores that site in round one. If this interferes with the source $t$ because its other neighbor is $p$, it delays the force into $y$ until round two. A loss on the other side of $t$ is restored from further along the complement, where the source can be the initially open $p$. As before a lost first neighbor off the cycle at $t$ is recoverable independently. All singleton losses finish.

**Dense single-path classification.** If every cycle vertex has a single pendant path, EveryMinimumSupport fails. For triangular cores and shallow attachments this was already proved. Consider the remaining graphs, and let $M$ be the maximum pendant length. If $M\ge3$, use the preceding local killing pair with a remote longest path and a triple of consecutive occupied vertices distinct from its site. This is the middle-site case, whose upper bound is $1+\lceil(M+1)/2\rceil\le M$ even for $M=3,4$. There is no bare vertex that could violate the recovery condition. If $M=2$ and the cycle has at least five vertices, choose a site $t$ of length two and the three sites preceding it. Then the first staggered construction applies with $P,X,Q\le2$. On the four-cycle the remaining length patterns up to symmetry are $(2,1,1,1)$, $(2,2,1,1)$, $(2,1,2,1)$, $(2,2,2,1)$ and $(2,2,2,2)$. The five explicit certificates in FOUR_CYCLE in verify_dense_path_sites.py cover them independently of the general constructions.

## A killing interval separated from a delayed interval

Let $a,y,t$ be consecutive cycle vertices. Suppose $a$ has a single pendant path of length $M\ge2$, $y$ is bare or has a single path of length at most $M$, and $t$ has no direct leaf. Color $y$ and the entire paths at $a,y$ white. Outside this interval, select an arc containing two other sites separated by one to three bare vertices, or three consecutive sites. Require at least two untouched ring vertices in both gaps between the two intervals. Let each site in the selected arc have a single path of length at most $2M-1$. All attachments at unselected sites are initially blue.

Use a killing pair in the selected arc as follows. For one bare vertex, use the local construction with the blue gate at the first site and the bare vertex in $H$. For two or three bare vertices, keep all of them blue initially and use the two endpoints of the bare interval as $H$. In this case both side paths start with white gates and blue even positions strictly before their terminals. With both losses the gates block both sides of the interval. With either single loss the other seeds restore the missing end in round one, so both side paths finish by $1+\lceil(2M)/2\rceil=1+M$. For three consecutive sites use the earlier middle-site construction, in which any missing internal ring vertex is restored in round one on a restricted loss. Its path times satisfy the same upper bound. Thus in all cases the restricted local completion time is at most $1+M$, and both losses kill on that arc regardless of the distant white interval.

In the remote interval, $t$ restores $y$ in round one on either restricted loss, and the path at $a$ finishes in round $1+M$. On loss of $t$, a blue exterior neighbor restores it in round one but $a$ cannot restore $y$ because its own gate is white. Then $t$ restores $y$ in round two and the path at $a$ finishes in round $2+M$. If another lost ring vertex interferes with $t$, it is restored from the next exterior blue vertex, since the two untouched vertices on that side keep it separate from the killing interval. A lost first off-cycle neighbor at $t$ has a blue child, and a lost $a$ is restored from its other ring neighbor on the guarded side. All losses in the local interval finish by its original singleton argument. Restoring any outside bare vertex cannot require a force from two blocked sites in different intervals, due to the two untouched vertices on both sides, and the only such bare vertex between the two local sites at distance two is a member of $H$. Off-cycle singleton losses either occur in the selected paths after their gates can be filled or recover after the ring colors return. The pair is therefore minimum and the two completion times differ.

In particular, if there are at least nine attachment sites and each attachment is a single path of length exactly two, the graph fails. The dense case is established above. Otherwise choose a site $a$ with a bare neighbor $y$. The next vertex $t$ has no leaf neighbor off the cycle. In the cyclic site list, set aside $a$ and the next site on the $t$ side, along with two sites on each outer side of this pair. At least three consecutive sites survive, and any arc they determine is separated by the required two ring vertices. Use the local arc with one through three bare vertices if available, the long-interval theorem if a gap has four or more bare vertices, or the three consecutive site case if neither gap contains a bare vertex. This proves the claimed exclusion for arbitrary placements with at least nine sites.


## Four-cycle configurations and a stronger fork comparison

Suppose the cycle is $p,x,q,t$ in order, with $t$ bare and with $x,q$ carrying single paths of lengths $X,Q$. At $p$ the child subtrees are one or more paths and at most one of them is a direct leaf. Select that leaf if present, or another path otherwise, and write $P$ for its length. On the selected path start with just its terminal blue. On every other path at $p$ start with odd positions strictly before the terminal blue. These other paths have length at least two. Color $p,x,t$ blue, $q$ white, and both paths at $x,q$ wholly white. Use $H$ consisting of $x$ and the selected terminal at $p$. Write $D=\max(X,Q)$ and let $W$ be the largest number of white vertices on an unselected path at $p$, or zero when there is no such path.

When both losses occur, $t$ restores $q$ but $p,q$ remain blocked by white $x$ and their respective white gates. The entirely white path at $x$ cannot restore it. When only $x$ is lost, the terminal at $p$ fills its head from below in $P-1$ rounds. The site $p$ then restores $x$ in round $P$, and the two paths at $x,q$ finish by $P+D$. The loss of the selected terminal alone permits the outward start from $p$, since its ring neighbors are blue, and $t$ restores $q$ in round one. In both restricted cases the unselected paths at $p$ use at most $W$ rounds. Hence the restricted time is exactly $\max(P+D,W)$.

If $p$ is lost, the head from its selected terminal restores it in exactly $P$ rounds. Its other initially blue first vertices cannot act into the white $p$, as each has a white successor. Until $p$ is blue, $t$ is blocked by two white ring neighbors and $x$ cannot restore $q$. The vertex $t$ restores $q$ in round $P+1$. The paths at $x,q$ finish in $P+1+D$ rounds and the other paths at $p$ take until $P+W$. Their maximum is strictly larger than the restricted time. If $t$ is lost, the inward front from the selected terminal at $p$ frees $p$ to restore $t$, and $t$ restores $q$. An unselected first vertex lost at $p$ may temporarily block $p$, but its selected terminal supplies the inward front and $t$ independently restores $q$. With the selected gate and both ring neighbors blue, the lost first vertex is restored. A deeper loss on an unselected path likewise does not prevent completion. All singleton losses finish. In particular this excludes every four-cycle with exactly three sites and a single path at each site.

**Comparison with an entirely white path.** In a unicyclic graph, suppose a cycle site $a$ has a single path of length $M$ and another center $u$ has two pendant paths, disjoint from that path and from $a$. If both lengths at $u$ are at most $2M-1$, color the path at $a$ wholly white. Put the first vertices of the two paths at $u$ in $H$ and use the first-vertex and even-position seeds on those paths as before. All remaining vertices, including the cycle, are blue. Losing both members leaves the two paths at $u$ permanently blocked. With one lost, $u$ restores its gate in round one and both paths finish by $M$, while the path at $a$ takes exactly $M$ rounds.

Lose a cycle neighbor $z$ of $a$ instead. Its other ring neighbor restores it in round one because that source has no other white neighbor. The path at $a$ must wait the first round and takes exactly $M+1$ rounds. The two paths at $u$ take at most $M$ rounds even if $z=u$, since each has its first vertex blue and no more than $M-1$ white vertices. For a singleton loss anywhere in the retained graph after removing the three selected paths, the only retained source that may be blocked by an initially white gate is $a$. A lost neighbor of $a$ has another ring neighbor, and every other lost vertex has a neighbor other than $a$. Thus the retained graph recovers by round one. Loss of a selected first vertex at $u$ is already covered, and a loss of another seed within one of those paths still permits the outward front. This proves minimum size two. The length condition is $2M-1$ here because the compared slow loss is a neighbor of $a$, and the length-$M$ path at $a$ starts with all its vertices white.

**A long path against opposite sites.** Let a four-cycle be $a,p,t,q$ with a nonpath attachment at $a$. Suppose the attachments at $p,q$ are each a single path or absent, and either $t$ has an attachment or at least one of the paths at $p,q$ has length at least two. Write $L$ for the maximum length at $p,q$ with zero for an absent path. If there is a descending pendant path of length $K>1+\lceil L/2\rceil$ from some $u$ in the attachment tree at $a$, use $H=\{p,q\}$. Start the whole cycle blue, color only the first vertex of the selected long path at $u$ blue, and use the odd internal positions excluding the terminal on the paths at $p,q$. Everything else is blue. Both members lost leave $a,t$ blocked and neither lost root has an autonomous source. Losing either one lets $a$ restore it in round one. The deadline is $\max(K-1,1+\lceil L/2\rceil)$. On loss of $u$, its retained parent or an unselected branch at $a$ restores it in round one, postponing its path to round $K$.

If $t$ is lost, it is restored by its fully blue attachment or by the ring neighbor whose nonleaf path head remains blue. If $a$ itself is lost while $u$ lies below it, the first vertex in the branch leading to $u$ can restore $a$, as the white part of the selected path starts at distance at least three from $a$. The other singleton losses leave their ring or recovered branch sources available, so the remaining vertices fill. This validates the minimum killing pair. As a consequence, on a four-cycle with at least three sites, at most one nonpath attachment, and a single-path site of length at least two, the graph fails. For the nonpath case, let $M$ be the largest length among single-path sites and take a deepest branching vertex with longest descending path $K$. When $K\le2M-1$ use the comparison with the wholly white path. Otherwise $K\ge2M>1+\lceil L/2\rceil$ and use the opposite pair. If $t$ is bare, both other sites besides $a$ are its ring neighbors, so $L=M\ge2$ as required.


## Complete classification on a four-cycle

For cycle length four the remaining graphs have exactly one nonpath attachment at $a$, at least two other sites, and exactly one direct leaf at each of those sites. Enumerate the cycle as $a,p,t,q$. By reversing the order we can assume that $p$ is a site. For a descending pendant path of length at least three at $a$ or below it, the earlier opposite-pair argument applies whenever $t$ is a site. Thus it remains to handle a bare $t$ with the two leaves at $p,q$, or to handle short descending paths.

**A bare opposite vertex and a descent of length $K\ge4$.** On the selected length-$K$ path at a vertex $u$ in the attachment tree at $a$, start with just its first vertex blue. All other vertices in that attachment are blue. Start with $a,t,q$ and the leaf at $p$ blue, with $p$ and the leaf of $q$ white. Take $H$ to consist of the leaf at $p$ and the site $q$. On loss of both, $p,q$ are white, neither can be restored by its own leaf and both sources $a,t$ see two white neighbors. On the loss of the leaf at $p$ alone, $t$ restores $p$ in round one. On loss of $q$ alone, the leaf at $p$ restores its own site in round one, then $t$ restores $q$ in round two and its leaf fills in round three. The selected arm at $u$ has finish time $K-1$ in both comparisons, so the restricted time is $K-1$.

If $u$ is lost, its initially blue arm head cannot advance until $u$ is restored. Its parent or an unselected fully blue branch restores it in round one, except that a direct child of $a$ with no other children may have to wait for $p$ to be restored before $a$ restores it in round two. In particular if $u=a$, its nonpath attachment supplies an unselected branch able to restore it. Hence the slow loss takes at least $K$ rounds. If $t$ is lost, the leaf at $p$ still restores $p$ in round one, after which $p$ can restore $t$. A lost vertex in another branch can be filled after the ring recovers, and the selected head can be restored from $u$ if it is lost instead. Thus all singleton losses finish.

**A short pair off the cycle.** Suppose an off-cycle branching vertex $u$ in the attachment tree at $a$ has two descending paths of length at most three, at least one of which has length two or three. Use their first vertices as $H$, with the first-vertex and even-position seeds. The center and its retained neighbors start blue, so either singleton loss in $H$ takes at most two rounds on these paths, and both losses kill.

If $p,q$ both have leaves, initially leave $p,q$ white and retain both leaves blue. This gives restoration of both sites in round one in each restricted comparison. Their first possible source into the other attachment at $a$ is irrelevant to the restoration at the off-cycle $u$. The loss of the leaf of $p$ lets $q$ restore itself from its own leaf in round one. The blue vertex $t$ then restores $p$ in round two and the lost leaf is restored in round three. If $a$ or $t$ is lost, the two sites restore themselves as usual and subsequently restore the missing vertex. An initially blue attachment at $t$, if present, introduces no earlier force into either white site when both are white. Every other singleton loss away from $p,q$ permits their simultaneous restoration, after which the recovered retained graph and the two arms can finish.

If $q$ is bare instead, leave $t$ and the leaves at $p,t$ white, with $a,p,q$ blue. In the restricted comparisons, $q$ restores $t$ in round one and the leaves at $p,t$ fill in round two. Losing $q$ postpones the force into $t$, since $p$ is blocked by its own white leaf. The vertex $a$ restores $q$ in round one, $q$ restores $t$ in round two, and the leaves fill in round three. If $a$ is lost, the initial blue first vertex on the branch toward $u$ restores $a$ in round one, since the two selected arm gates at $u$ are blue on this particular loss. If $p$ is lost, $a$ can restore it and $q$ is still available to restore $t$. A loss within the retained attachment at $a$ leaves the ring recovery available, and the selected path fronts complete once their gates are blue. All singleton losses finish.

**Exhaustion.** If no long descending path is available, take a deepest branching vertex of the nonpath attachment. Its children lie on paths of length at most three. In a twin-free graph at most one path has length one. If this vertex is off the cycle, the short pair argument applies. If it is $a$ itself, retain two of the paths of lengths at most three, retaining its direct leaf if present. Up to symmetry the two lengths are $(1,2),(1,3),(2,2),(2,3),(3,3)$ and the other sites are $(1,3),(1,2)$ or $(1,2,3)$ when $a=0$. The fifteen explicit certificates AT_ROOT in verify_four_cycle_complete.py replay on exactly these cores. In each certificate the vertex $a$ is blue by round one in the intact, two restricted, double-loss and slow colorings. All omitted branches have nonleaf first vertices. The proved all-blue attachment extension therefore gives the same deadlines for arbitrary remaining branches of this form. Together with the earlier two-site, single-path and twin cases, this gives the full four-cycle classification. A unicyclic graph with a four-cycle as its core satisfies EveryMinimumSupport exactly when it is the pure four-cycle.


## Complete classification for paths of length at most two

Suppose every site has a single pendant path of length at most two, and at least one has length two. Such a graph fails EveryMinimumSupport for every cycle length and every site placement.

For at least nine sites, a run of three occupied cycle vertices gives the staggered construction at the end of the run, where its next vertex is bare. If the run extends all the way around the ring, use the dense classification. With runs of length at most two, choose a length-two site $a$. If its run has length two, let $y$ be its mate and let $t$ be the following bare vertex. This supplies the remote delayed interval. If $a$ is isolated between two bare neighbors, it supplies the same interval whenever one of the two second vertices is bare or has a length-two path. In either case the remote interval covers at most two occupied sites. Reserve its sites and the nearest additional sites needed for two untouched ring vertices on both sides. It suffices to set aside $a$ and one adjacent site in the cyclic site list, together with the two sites beyond this pair in each direction. At least three consecutive sites remain. Select a short killing interval among them. A positive bare gap of one through three vertices, or three consecutively occupied ring vertices, gives the separated-interval construction. A gap of four or more instead gives the long bare interval construction.

In the only remaining configuration each length-two site $t$ is isolated and separated by one bare vertex from a length-one site on each side. Use $a,y,t$ as the remote interval, where the site $a$ has length one and $y$ is bare. Its leaf and $y$ start white. The remote time is two under either restricted loss, because $t$ restores $y$ in round one. Loss of $t$ postpones the force into $y$ until round two, and the leaf at $a$ takes three rounds. The same two-vertex guards used in the separated-interval construction ensure that every other singleton loss finishes. Now the killing interval needs a two-round deadline. Reserve the two sites $a,t$ and the two sites beyond them on either side. If a surviving triple of consecutive sites contains a length-two site, it includes a pair of lengths two and one with a single bare vertex between them. Put in $H$ the initially blue first vertex of the length-two path and the initially blue bare vertex. The leaf of the length-one site starts white. Losing both leaves a blocked interval, and either single loss restores the missing vertex in round one and finishes the remaining path or leaf in round two. If all sites in the surviving triple are short, some pair has a positive bare gap because there are no runs of length three. One bare vertex uses the same construction with a blue leaf in place of the length-two first vertex. Two or three bare vertices use the two gap endpoints in $H$, with the whole gap initially blue and the two side leaves white, so the restricted time is again two. A gap of four or more uses the earlier long bare interval theorem. This completes the proof for at least nine sites.

For three through eight sites, cores of length three and four are already classified. If any gap has four or more bare vertices, the long interval theorem applies, except if its complementary arc has only one internal vertex. In that exception there are exactly three consecutively occupied sites on the complementary arc, and the staggered interval applies at the bare end. Thus for the finite reduction every cyclic gap is in $\{0,1,2,3\}$. The gap sequence and the site lengths determine the graph up to dihedral symmetry. For uniform length two, verify_length_two_complete.py checks 6,373 gap words, including the seventeen explicit witnesses in RESIDUAL. For mixed lengths, verify_short_path_mixtures_complete.py checks every nonconstant length assignment modulo the stabilizer of its canonical gap word. It covers 1,221,014 decorated gap words. All except 161 match a proved interval construction. For the remaining cases the same script replays a budget-one certificate and stores it in short-path-mixtures-complete-replay.json. The finite search uses white sets consisting of a cycle-arc fort with two selected vertices made blue and one additional white outside the fort. The classification uses the checked certificates, since survival of all singleton losses and the strict time gap were verified for each stored witness. Together with the one-site and two-site theorems, this proves the assertion for all site counts.

## Six or more single-path sites with arbitrary lengths

For a simple longest path of length $M\ge3$ at $a$, two short bare gaps can be used with symmetric gates. Suppose $p,q$ have paths of lengths at most $M$, and are separated on an arc by two or three bare vertices. Require that neither $p$ nor $q$ is nearest to $a$ in either direction in the cyclic list of sites. Start with all ring vertices blue, the path at $a$ all white, and the paths at $p,q$ each with white first vertex and even internal positions blue, excluding their terminals. Use the first and last bare vertices of the displayed gap as $H$.

After both losses the two endpoint sites are blocked by their path gates and the missing ends of the gap. Any middle blue vertex is isolated. With one lost, the other bare vertices restore it in round one. The two paths are finished by $1+\lceil(M+1)/2\rceil\le M$, while the longest path at $a$ takes $M$ rounds. After any other singleton ring loss an adjacent source is available unless a bare vertex lies between two blocked sites. The internal gap does not create such a bare vertex, and its complement includes other sites. Neither endpoint is nearest to $a$, so neither can share a bare neighbor with $a$. Any other occupied vertex is restored from its initially blue attachment if needed. Recovery of an off-cycle loss uses the intact ring and eventual outward fronts. A slow loss of either cycle neighbor of $a$ forces its path to wait at least one round. This gives the minimum killing pair and completes the symmetric short-gap case.

For at least six single-path sites, delete $a$ and its two nearest sites in the cyclic site list. At least three consecutive sites remain. If two of them have a gap of one bare vertex, the earlier local killing pair applies with the bound $1+\lceil(M+1)/2\rceil\le M$. For two or three bare vertices use the symmetric gates just proved. A gap of at least four uses the long interval theorem. If neither gap is positive, the three sites are consecutive ring vertices and the earlier middle-site construction applies with the same one-round upper bound on its local delay. All selected sites are separated from $a$ as required. For maximum length at most two the classification is already complete, including the shallow case. Thus every graph with at least six single-path sites and without any nonpath attachment fails at arbitrary order.

The same longest-path argument applies with one nonpath attachment and at least nine sites, whenever a single-path site has length at least three. After deleting $a$, its two nearest sites and the possible nonpath site, at least five sites survive in at most two runs. One run has three sites. The nonpath attachment is otherwise fully blue and does not affect the local recovery argument. The case of at least two nonpath attachments was already settled.


## Completing the single-path classification by extending terminal segments

Let a finite connected graph have a budget-one certificate $(B,H,z)$ with $|H|=2$. On a collection of disjoint pendant paths, allow additional vertices beyond the original terminal leaves. The added paths can be of arbitrary nonnegative lengths $d_i$. Use either of the following choices at a terminal $v_i$.

- A wholly blue extension is neutral for the five compared colorings $B$, the two restricted singleton losses, the double loss $H$, and the slow loss $z$, provided that $v_i$ is blue by round one in each of those original colorings. Impose one further condition for a loss of a sole newly added leaf. If $v_i$ is initially blue and its original neighbor is white, require that the intact original coloring still fills when forces from $v_i$ are suppressed.
- A wholly white extension is allowed at a terminal that is initially white. The old process is unchanged, and the added path needs exactly $d_i$ rounds after $v_i$ is restored in each successful comparison.

For the blue extension, the first attached blue vertex can help restore $v_i$ in round one, which is already blue by that round in the five compared original processes. Projection onto the old graph agrees from then on. Other old singleton losses can only benefit from the attached blue vertices. If the first new vertex of a longer blue tail is lost, its own blue successor restores it in round one. Loss of a deeper vertex leaves an initially blue first vertex. The old graph fills, and a sole remaining lost vertex is restored. If the sole new leaf is lost, an initially white $v_i$ can still be restored by exactly its ordinary process. An initially blue $v_i$ with an initially blue old neighbor has no need to force into the old graph. In the remaining case the suppression condition supplies completion. The white extension cannot influence an old force. Before the terminal is restored it has no source along the new path. After it is restored, its old neighbor is already blue, so its new white neighbor does not impede any old force. These arguments allow all blue and white extensions simultaneously, since they use disjoint terminal paths and each loss outside the old graph is a singleton.

Let $R,S$ be the original restricted and slow completion times. For each white extension let $r_i$ be the maximum time when $v_i$ becomes blue among the intact and two restricted colorings, and let $s_i$ be its arrival time under the slow loss. The two times after extension are exactly

$$
\max\bigl(R,\max_i(r_i+d_i)\bigr),\qquad
\max\bigl(S,\max_i(s_i+d_i)\bigr).
$$

In particular, if $a$ is an index known to have a largest increment, the strict difference persists if for every white extension $i$ either $s_i>r_i$, or $a$ also has a white extension and $s_a>r_i$. The proof is term by term. The original comparison gives $S>R$. Each extended restricted term is strictly smaller than the applicable extended slow term by the arrival inequality and, in the second option, by $d_a\ge d_i$.

It remains to use this extension criterion on the finitely many bare gap patterns for three through five single-path sites. All lengths can start at one for a leaf and at two for any path of length at least two. The extensions from length two have a common base, so any longest marked path has a largest increment. For every canonical layout and possible marked longest path, the code verify_path_padding_reduction.py gives a certificate that passes the extension criterion, except for one layout and mark. This exception has gap vector $(0,1,1)$ and minimum lengths $(1,1,2)$, with the third path marked. Length two there is covered by the earlier short-path theorem. When the same path has length at least three, the supplied certificate uses minimum length three and allows every further extension. No more length subdivisions occur.

The verifier verify_path_padding_coverage.py independently enumerates the dihedral classes, checks every stored certificate and every terminal condition, and checks that the covered longest marks and the one subdivision exhaust the initial length cases. It checks 4,050 initial layouts and 5,087 base certificates. By the arithmetic criterion, this is a finite verification of the full unbounded length family. With the previously proved site count and long gap reductions, it follows that any finite connected unicyclic graph with at least one attachment and a single pendant path at every site fails EveryMinimumSupport, except the already identified shallow positive triangles.

## Four or more sites with a branching attachment

Assume a twin-free unicyclic graph with cycle length at least five, at least four sites, and exactly one nonpath attachment at $a$. The sites other than $a$ each have a single path. Write $M$ for their maximum length.

**Using a long arm.** Suppose a descending pendant path of length $K\ge\max(3,2M)$ starts at a vertex $u$ in the attachment tree at $a$. Make just its first vertex blue and leave its tail white. Choose three consecutive sites other than $a$ in the cyclic site order. If a gap between them has at least four bare vertices, the long interval theorem applies. Otherwise use one of the established local killing pairs on this arc, with an open first gate for one bare vertex, both gap endpoints for two or three bare vertices, or the middle-site construction for three adjacent sites. The rest of the cycle and the retained parts of the trees start blue. With a loss in that pair, the local completion time is at most $1+\lceil(M+1)/2\rceil\le K-1$, and the arm at $u$ fills in $K-1$ rounds. Losing both support vertices leaves the local arc permanently blocked. On loss of $u$, its retained parent, or an unselected branch when $u=a$, restores it in round one. The long arm therefore takes $K$ rounds. The local arm palettes do not block the recovery of any remaining outside singleton. In the two-wall case the displayed gap has at least two bare vertices and its complement contains $a$, so no other bare vertex is trapped between the two blocked local sites. For a singleton on an outside retained branch all local sources remain available, and after the local arc returns the selected long path and the remaining branch recover. An initially white portion of the long arm lies behind a blue first vertex and introduces no further obstruction to the ring. Thus all singleton losses finish.

If $M\ge2$, take a deepest branching vertex and its longest descending path of length $K$. For $K\le2M-1$ use the fork comparison with a wholly white path of length $M$. Otherwise the long arm comparison applies. It remains only to handle graphs whose other sites are short leaves and whose nonpath attachment has no descending pendant path of length at least three.

**Two short leaf clocks.** With the two initial arm heads blue at a fork with paths of lengths one and two, use those heads as $H$. The first-loss restricted time at the fork is two if the retained neighbors of the fork are initially blue. Use either of the following exterior colorings. All unspecified vertices are blue.

1. In three consecutive cycle positions $q,y,t$, let $q$ have a direct leaf, let $y$ be bare or have a direct leaf, and let $t$ have no direct leaf. Leave $y$, the leaf at $q$, and the leaf at $y$ if present white. The vertex $t$ restores $y$ in round one under either restricted loss. The leaves turn blue in round two. Loss of $t$ delays $y$ until round two and the leaves until round three. In an outside singleton loss, a lost other cycle neighbor of $t$ can be restored from its own other side. If a direct neighbor of $t$ within a tree is lost, it has a child that is blue and restores it, except for the loss of the fork itself when adjacent to $t$, in which case its own short leaf restores it. Loss of $q$ is recoverable from its other ring neighbor.
2. In four consecutive positions $r,p,t,q$, let $p,q$ each have one leaf, and let $r$ be bare or carry only one leaf. Leave $p,q$ white and their leaves blue. If $r$ is bare leave it white, and otherwise leave just its leaf white. The two blue leaves restore $p,q$ in round one. If needed $p$ restores the remaining bare $r$ in round two, or $r$ restores its own leaf. Loss of the leaf of $p$ leaves both sources $r,t$ unable to restore $p$ in round one. The site $q$ is restored from its leaf, then $t$ restores $p$ in round two. A bare $r$ is recovered from its other neighbor by round two at the latest. The lost leaf waits until round three. Loss of the leaf at $q$ permits $p$ to restore itself first and $t$ to restore $q$. If $r$ or $t$ is lost, $p,q$ restore themselves and can then act into the lost vertex. In particular the other neighbor of $q$ lies outside $r,p,t,q$ since the cycle has length at least five. A different outside loss does not remove either self-restoring leaf. The ring and any remaining leaf complete once $p,q$ are blue.

In these arguments, when the fork lies on the ring require that none of its ring neighbors starts white and that it is not the source $t$. Its initially blue short leaf restores it on its own singleton loss. If the fork lies off the cycle, these restrictions on the exterior coloring are unnecessary. Its retained parent starts blue and its two arm gates do not meet the exterior clock. On a loss at the root $a$ above it, an initially blue branch into its retained subtree supplies restoration. The singleton tests from both clocks then complete with the two arms. Thus their loss delays of two versus three give a minimum-pair certificate also in the full graph.

**Exhaustion for at least four sites.** In the case $M=1$ with all descending pendant paths of length at most two, two incomparable branching vertices with two pendant paths each give the earlier two-center obstruction. If a deepest one $u$ is off the cycle, the common-center obstructions reduce its lengths to exactly $(1,2)$. With a branching ancestor, choose the nearest such ancestor $p$ and use the short-fork stem cases. The case not already specified there is when $p=a$ is on the cycle, it is adjacent to $u$, and its side branches have length two. The adjacent length-two side-path certificate works here as well. On singleton losses outside the selected paths at least one of the two cycle neighbors of the initially white $p$ is available to restore it in round one. The two neighbors are nonadjacent and have no common second neighbor on a cycle of length at least five. A loss in a leaf at one of them cannot interfere with the other. This proves the needed exterior recovery in that certificate. If $u$ has no branching ancestor, its stem top has no direct leaf. When two short sites are adjacent, take the last pair in a maximal consecutive run of short sites. The next vertex is bare or is that stem top, so the first leaf clock applies. If no two short sites are adjacent, use three in cyclic order after omitting $a$. A gap of two or more bare vertices permits the first clock. If both gaps have one bare vertex, the second clock uses the middle and last sites as $p,q$ and the bare before $p$ as $r$.

If $u=a$ has all child paths long, the ring common-center obstruction applies. For lengths five and six the required attachment at distance two exists because there are at least four sites. If it has one short path and at least two long paths, the one-short common-center argument applies. The only remaining root has exactly $(1,2)$. For at least six sites, delete $a$ and its two nearest sites in the cyclic site order. Choose three consecutive survivors. Any gap of two or more gives the first clock, a gap of one gives the second clock with intervening bare $t$, and two zero gaps let the first and third sites serve as $p,q$ with the middle site as $t$. The preceding vertex $r$ is bare or a short site and is distinct from $a$. A bare $r$ cannot be adjacent to $a$ because $p$ is not one of the nearest sites to $a$. Hence both clock colorings meet the root restrictions.

For four or five sites this last root $(1,2)$ and all other sites short has a bounded reduction. A bare gap of at least four uses the long interval theorem, selecting the short leaf at $a$ if an endpoint is $a$. The additional length-two neighbor is nonleaf. The other arc has at least two internal vertices. With all gaps at most three, the graph is specified by the gap word beginning at $a$ up to reversal at $a$. The verification in verify_four_or_five_fork_sites.py checks all 679 resulting layouts on cycles of length at least five. The leaf clocks cover 677, and the other two have explicit replayed certificates in the associated report. Thus every graph with at least four sites, a cycle of length at least five, and one nonpath attachment is excluded in arbitrary order.


## Reductions for the last three-site case

Let $a$ be the nonpath site and $b,c$ the single-path sites of a twin-free graph with exactly three sites and cycle length at least five. If $M=\max(L_b,L_c)\ge2$ and the longest descending paths at a deepest branching vertex of the $a$ attachment have length at most $2M-1$, the wholly white path comparison applies. More generally it applies whenever any branching vertex has two qualifying descending paths.

If a descending arm of length $K\ge\max(3,2M)$ exists, the long arm comparison does not require three exterior single-path sites when the arc from $b$ to $c$ avoiding $a$ already has one, two or three bare vertices. That interval alone gives the local killing pair. The complementary arc contains $a$, so the singleton recovery condition for the two-wall case still holds. If the indicated interval has at least four bare vertices and the complementary arc has at least two internal vertices, the previously proved long bare interval theorem applies without using the arm at $a$. Thus in this length range the layouts still needing attention have either adjacent single-path sites or a consecutive run $b,a,c$ with at least four bare vertices on its other arc.

When both $b,c$ are short leaves and every descending pendant path within the $a$ attachment has length at most two, the classification is complete. Incomparable terminal forks and the common-center obstructions reduce the off-cycle case to a unique deepest pair of lengths $(1,2)$. Any branching ancestor is excluded by the side-path and side-leaf comparisons below, which apply also to $K=2$. This leaves a simple stem to the fork. The exterior leaf clocks then work with just two short sites. If $b,c$ are adjacent, their run ends at a bare vertex or at the stem top, and that next vertex has no direct leaf. If their arc avoiding $a$ has two or more bare vertices, its first two bare vertices give the first clock. If this arc has exactly one bare vertex, use the second clock with $b,c$ as $p,q$. At least one orientation makes the preceding $r$ different from $a$, since otherwise $b,c$ are the two neighbors of $a$ on a four-cycle. That $r$ is bare.

When the fork lies at $a$, two or more long arms are covered by the ring common-center argument, except for cycles of length five or six with the other sites at the two neighbors of $a$. In those two cases use the explicit two-length-two-arm certificates ALL_LONG in verify_three_short_fork_sites.py and attach any extra fully blue nonleaf arms, using the verified first-round condition at $a$. If $a$ has a short leaf and at least two long arms, the one-short common-center result applies. This leaves just the root pair $(1,2)$. A bare gap of length at least four permits the long interval theorem unless all three sites are consecutive on its complement. In that exceptional layout there are two types up to reflection, according to whether $a$ is at an end or in the middle of the occupied run. In both cases an explicit five-cycle certificate has a blue edge in the bare gap, so the blue-edge subdivision lemma covers every longer bare gap. The five-cycle certificates are in CONSECUTIVE in verify_three_short_fork_sites.py. For gaps all at most three, the same verifier checks the 37 gap words modulo reversal at $a$ by direct certificate replay. This completes the short case at three sites.

### A bare arc opposite the branching site

Suppose $b,a,c$ are consecutive and the other arc from $b$ to $c$ has $g\ge4$ bare vertices. Write $L_b\le L_c=M$ after reflection. At a vertex $u$ of the $a$ attachment, choose a descending pendant arm of length $K\ge\max(3,2M)$. Color its first vertex blue and its tail white. All other vertices at $a$, including $a$ itself, start blue. The two candidate loss vertices are $H=\{b,c\}$. On each exterior path, color the odd positions strictly before the terminal vertex blue and the other positions white. In particular a path of length one starts wholly white.

Choose the colors on the bare arc as follows.

- If $L_b,L_c\ge2$, make the arc wholly white when $g$ is odd. When $g$ is even, put a blue seed at the second bare vertex from $b$. Let $W$ be the resulting odd number of white bare vertices. With one loss in $H$, the two fronts enter the arc in rounds one and two, and the bare arc completes in $\lceil(W+1)/2\rceil$ rounds. The isolated seed, if present, reduces the number of white vertices along a front by one. Losing $a$ delays both fronts to round two because both boundary sites initially see two white cycle neighbors. The $a$ attachment restores $a$ in round one. The bare arc now takes $1+\lceil W/2\rceil$ rounds.
- If $L_b=1<L_c$, make the first bare vertex from $b$ blue and the others white. Under either singleton in $H$, the bare arc fills within $\lceil g/2\rceil$ rounds because its fronts begin in rounds one and two. Losing that first bare seed blocks $b$ on its leaf and its bare neighbor. The front from $c$ traverses all $g$ bare vertices, then the leaf at $b$ fills in round $g+1$.
- If both lengths are one, make the first and last bare vertices blue and leave the others white. One singleton loss in $H$ permits the two fronts to enter the remaining bare arc in rounds one and two or earlier. The arc and both leaves finish by round $\max(2,\lfloor g/2\rfloor)$. Losing the first bare seed from $b$ forces the front from $c$ to fill $g-1$ bare vertices, and then the leaf at $b$ fills in round $g$.

For the double loss, $a$ sees two white cycle neighbors. Any blue bare seed has two white neighbors, and the odd path seeds cannot independently restore a missing site because the first odd seed also has a white successor. Thus $H$ kills. Under a singleton in $H$ the lost site is restored in round one, and the long exterior gates fill within $1+\lceil M/2\rceil\le K-1$ rounds. The arm at $u$ takes $K-1$ rounds. If this arm is at least as slow as the bound for the arc and its leaves, use the loss of $u$ for the unrestricted comparison. A blue neighbor outside the arm restores $u$ in round one and the arm then takes $K-1$ further rounds. Otherwise use the delayed loss from the corresponding arc construction.

Every singleton completes. Losing an initial gate at $b$ or $c$ can block its own site, but the other front still traverses the bare arc and frees the affected site. The same argument handles the loss of an optional seed on a short side. Loss of the isolated seed in the first case leaves both fronts available. Loss of $a$ permits its restoration from a retained branch or stem, after which any stalled front starts. A singleton within the $a$ attachment leaves the ring fronts available. Once the ring is blue, a lost vertex outside the selected arm is restored from a retained neighbor, using the first blue arm vertex or another branch when the lost vertex is $u$. If the first arm vertex is itself lost, $u$ can restore it when its retained neighbors are blue. The arm and the exterior path tails then finish.

### Adjacent single-path sites and long bare arcs

Orient the cycle as $a$, then $L$ bare vertices, then $b,c$, then $R$ bare vertices and back to $a$. There are certificates when both gaps have length at least three, or when one gap has length at most two and the other at least thirteen. In the first regime orient it so $L\ge R$. In the second orient it so $L\le2$ and $R\ge13$. Again choose a descending arm of length $K\ge\max(3,2M)$ at $u$. Only its first vertex starts blue, and all other off-cycle vertices on the $a$ side start blue.

Leave $a$ white and color the last three bare vertices before $a$ blue. Call their two outer vertices $H$ and their middle vertex $z$. Leave one of $b,c$, denoted $v$, white and the other blue with its whole path blue. Take $v=b$ when $L=0$, or when both gaps have length at least three, $R>3$, and $L_b\ge2$. In all other cases take $v=c$. On the path at $v$, color only the odd positions strictly before its end blue. All remaining bare vertices are white at this stage.

The $a$ attachment restores $a$ in round one in every loss confined to the cycle. If $u=a$, at least one other branch can restore $a$. If $u$ is off the cycle, the first vertex of the stem has all its neighbors other than $a$ blue. With both members of $H$ missing, $a$ sees two white cycle neighbors, the middle seed is isolated, and the initially blue one of $b,c$ also sees two white cycle neighbors. The latter condition uses $v=b$ for $L=0$ and $v=c$ for $R=3$. The gates at $v$ cannot start a force. This traps the original white cycle vertices and the two losses.

Under a single loss in $H$, the leftward front from $a$ enters the remaining arc in round two. The rightward front starts in round two if its outer marker was lost and in round one otherwise. A missing outer marker is restored in round one by the middle seed. Under loss of $z$ the outer markers each initially see two white neighbors. The marker next to $a$ restores $z$ in round two after $a$ is blue. The leftward front still starts in round two and the rightward front begins in round three.

Usually the interval between these fronts is handled by parity. It contains $L+R-2$ white cycle vertices, counting $v$. If this is odd, add a blue seed at the second bare vertex from $a$ on the length-$L$ side when $L,R\ge3$, or at the second bare vertex from $c$ on the length-$R$ side when $L\le2$. The added seed has two white neighbors and is outside the three markers. Write $\ell,r$ for the resulting numbers of white bare vertices on the two sides and $W=\ell+r+1$ for the resulting positive even total. A front absorbs the extra seed as soon as it fills one of its neighbors. The worst restricted pair of starting rounds is $(2,2)$ and gives cycle deadline $1+W/2$. The slow pair $(2,3)$ leaves at least one cycle vertex white at that deadline. An initially blue site may add a blue step to the interval, but crossing it consumes no extra round since the site can act as soon as its incoming neighbor turns blue. The time at $v$ under the worst restricted starts is $1+\min(\ell+1,r+1)$, and its path then takes $\lceil L_v/2\rceil$ more rounds.

For $L,R\ge3$, the rightward front first reaches $v$ because $r+1=R-2$ and $\ell+1\ge L\ge R$. This order persists after a one-round delay of the rightward front, so the path at $v$ is also delayed one round. If $v$ has a white leaf, then $v=c$, and the incoming rightward front finds its other neighbor $b$ blue. The front can continue from $b$ independently of the leaf. On the one-short-side configurations, whenever the parity construction is used the deadline of the path at $v$ is at most $L+2+\lceil M/2\rceil$. This is at most the cycle deadline for $M\le3$, since $R\ge13$ and $1+W/2\ge6$. For $M\ge4$ it is at most $2M-1$, hence bounded by the selected arm deadline. If $L=0$ and $v=b$ has a leaf, the left side restores $b$ first and the other cycle neighbor $c$ is already blue.

The exception to the parity construction is $1\le L\le2$, $R\ge13$, and $v=c$ with a white leaf. Use no extra seed. The leftward front reaches $c$ by round $L+2$. The leaf blocks $c$ from entering the other gap until its last white neighbor there is blue. For the worst restricted loss the rightward front fills the $R-3$ bare vertices by round $R-2$ and the leaf by round $R-1$. Losing the middle marker delays both of these deadlines by one.

The selected arm finishes under either restricted loss in $U=K-1+\mathbf 1_{u=a}$ rounds. If it is at least as slow as the exterior, use the loss of its first vertex for the slow comparison. It cannot recover before round one for $u$ off-cycle, or before round two for $u=a$. The entire arm must then fill from its stem end, giving a delay of at least one round. This loss is survivable because the three bare markers remain blue, so the marker next to $a$ can restore $a$ if needed. If $u=a$ and the missing arm blocks the leftward front, the rightward front can travel around the cycle to free $a$. The choice of $v$ ensures that if its path is a white leaf, it has a blue neighbor on the far side from the first front, or is next to $a$ and both its neighbors are restored by the rightward front. Its leaf therefore cannot stop the only available front indefinitely.

The remaining singleton losses also complete. Outside the three markers, the intact triplet permits fronts from both ends of the arc after $a$ is restored. If an initially blue site is lost, its blue attached path restores it. If a path gate is lost, a stalled front can be met from the other direction so that both cycle neighbors of its site turn blue. A missing internal path seed wakes from a surviving neighbor or from the filled cycle. Loss of the optional isolated seed only adds one white bare vertex to the traversal. Off-cycle losses in the $a$ attachment are restored once the cycle is full, using the intact triplet if the original restoring force into $a$ was obstructed. Thus $H$ is minimum and the chosen unrestricted loss is strictly slower.

### Restricting the attachment at the remaining nonpath site

In the remaining adjacent-site configurations, two incomparable deepest branching vertices are excluded by the two-center obstruction. With a unique deepest branching vertex $u$, every ancestor has only pendant paths to the side of the stem from $a$ to $u$. The common-center constructions reduce the arms at $u$ to one leaf and one long arm. If $u=a$ and its cycle length is five or six, some occupied site is at cycle distance two from $a$ because $b,c$ are adjacent. This supplies the occupied distance-two case of the common-center construction for two long arms. If an ancestor has at least two side paths, its paths and those at $u$ give the two-center obstruction, including the compressed and adjacent cases.

Assume there is just one side path of length $J$ at an ancestor $p$. Write $K$ for the length of the long terminal arm at $u$. If $J\ge\lceil(K+1)/2\rceil$, leave the side path at $p$ wholly white. At $u$ retain its short leaf and the first vertex of the long arm as $H$, and retain the even positions of the long arm strictly before its end. All other vertices start blue. After loss of both candidates the two white gates at $u$ stay white. With either lost alone, the gate is restored in round one and the long arm completes by $\lceil(K+1)/2\rceil$, while the side path finishes in exactly $J$ rounds. Losing the neighbor of $p$ toward the cycle, or a cycle neighbor if $p=a$, delays the side path by one round. This neighbor is restored in round one from the retained side toward the rest of the graph. For any other loss among the wholly blue skeleton, the only persistent gate at a skeleton vertex is at $p$. A lost neighbor of $p$ can be restored from another neighbor, using the intact short leaf if it is $u$. A loss of $p$ itself is restored from above it. Loss of an additional seed in the long arm leaves the retained stem and terminal fork available for eventual traversal. Hence the pair is minimum and the delayed side path completes in round $J+1$.

Now suppose that a side leaf occurs and choose the nearest ancestor $p$ of $u$ with a side leaf. If $p$ is adjacent to $u$, use the adjacent side-leaf stem coloring from the short-fork stem comparison. It uses the two leaves as $H$, makes $u$ and the long arm tail white, and gives times $K$ and $K+1$. Otherwise set $m=\operatorname{dist}(p,u)\ge2$, make the interior stem white, and use the two leaves at $p,u$ as $H$. On intervening side paths retain just the odd positions strictly before the end. Such a side path has length $J$ between two and $\lfloor K/2\rfloor$ by the choice of $p$ and the long-side comparison. The long arm at $u$ retains its first vertex only. The remainder of the graph is blue. The double loss traps the white stem because its ends each see a lost leaf and a white stem neighbor, and the side paths cannot wake a white stem vertex. Under each restricted loss a single stem front fills the interior and restores the lost leaf in round $m$. The long tail fills in $K-1$ rounds. An intervening stem vertex at distance $d$ from $u$ is restored by round $\max(d,m-d)$ across the two losses, so its side path finishes by round $\max(d,m-d)+\lceil J/2\rceil$. The largest of these bounds is strictly less than $m+K-1$. Losing the first vertex of the long arm instead requires the front from $p$ to reach the neighbor of $u$ before $u$ can restore the arm, which finishes in round $m+K-1$ or later. A loss of either stem endpoint is repaired by its retained leaf. With a loss on an intervening side path, the two fronts can reach either side of the affected stem vertex independently, and that vertex then restores the gate. The remaining singleton losses leave the needed fronts or are restored from the retained portion above $p$. This excludes side leaves.

Consequently the attachment at $a$ has a stem, possibly of length zero, to a terminal fork with lengths $(1,K)$. Each proper ancestor of the fork has at most one pendant side path and its length, when present, lies between two and $\lfloor K/2\rfloor$.

### Finite certificates for the remaining fork and adjacent sites

Use bare gap sizes $(L,R)$ with $L\le R$, $L\le2$, $R\le12$, and $L+R\ge2$. For each exterior path use base length one if it is a leaf and two otherwise. Use base length $K_0=3$ on the long fork arm when both exterior paths are leaves and $K_0=4$ otherwise. The long arm is indexed by zero and the exterior paths by one and two. If their increments are $d_i$, then $d_0\ge d_i$ for every growing exterior path. Indeed $K\ge2M$ gives $K-4\ge M-2$ when $M\ge2$. There are 134 ring and base-length assignments after reflection. On each assignment consider the five bare stem lengths $0,1,2,3,4$. Length four represents every larger stem length by subdivision of a designated initially blue stem edge.

Each of the 670 bases has a budget-one certificate $(B,H,z)$ and one of the two previously proved terminal extension types for each growing arm. In each of the five compared colorings, a terminal assigned an all-blue extension is blue by round one. A terminal assigned a white extension is initially white. For each white terminal $i$, write $r_i$ for its last arrival among the intact and two restricted cases, and $s_i$ for its arrival after the slow loss. The certificates satisfy $s_i>r_i$ or $s_0>r_i$ with a white extension at terminal zero. Since $d_0\ge d_i$, each extended restricted term $r_i+d_i$ is strictly below the extended slow time.

A side path of length $J$ at a proper ancestor $p$ is attached as follows.

- If $p$ is blue by round one in all five comparisons, add an all-blue path. Its first vertex can only accelerate $p$ to round one, so the old comparisons are unchanged. Any singleton loss within the added path is repaired in round one or leaves the gate initially blue and finishes after the old graph fills.
- Otherwise add a path with just its odd positions strictly before the terminal vertex blue. Its first vertex is blue and cannot force while $p$ is white, because its successor is also white. The other blue vertices on this path each initially have two white neighbors. Thus the path has no effect on the old graph in any comparison. Once $p$ turns blue, its side path takes $\lceil J/2\rceil$ rounds. If the first vertex of an added side path is lost, the base certificate guarantees that the intact old coloring fills even if all forces from $p$ are suppressed. Once the old graph is blue, $p$ can restore its gate and finish the side path. Any other singleton loss in the added path leaves the gate blue.

For the second choice let $r_p$ be the latest arrival of $p$ in the intact and restricted cases, and $s_p$ its arrival after the slow loss. Each base certificate satisfies $s_p>r_p$ or $s_0>r_p+1$ with a white extension at terminal zero. In the first case the side path completes strictly later in the slow loss. In the second case its restricted deadline is at most $r_p+1+d_0<s_0+d_0$, since

$$
\left\lceil J/2\right\rceil\le 1+d_0
\qquad\text{whenever}\qquad 2\le J\le\left\lfloor(K_0+d_0)/2\right\rfloor.
$$

The side-path extensions and the terminal extensions can be made together. Every intact added gate at a proper ancestor is blue, so the projections on the base graph in the five comparisons are as described. For an old singleton loss, the only new forces into the base graph can accelerate it. For a new singleton loss, each required completion or suppression argument applies on the base graph. After the base fills, all added paths fill. Therefore the two-loss coloring still fails and every singleton loss succeeds. The old restricted time and all added restricted terms are strictly below the slow time.

For a longer stem, subdivide the designated blue edge and use an all-blue side path at each new vertex when required. Both endpoints of the edge are retained blue in all five comparisons, and at least one has all neighbors blue in $B$. The previously proved blue-edge subdivision applies even for a single inserted vertex. Any added side path at an old endpoint has an initially blue first vertex, so the condition remains valid. This extends the length-four cases to every stem length at least four.

The certificate file adjacent-fork-exploration-03.json provides the 670 bases. The independent verifier verify_adjacent_fork_bases.py reconstructs the base graphs and the complete set of required parameters. It checks the one-loss, two-loss, timing, suppression, and edge conditions. It also checks 2,010 explicit extended instances with the pinned simulator. The arbitrary-length conclusion follows from the extension inequalities and subdivision. This completes the last exclusion family.

## Two successive cycle intervals with leaf sites
**Proposition.** Let $a,b,w$ occur in this order around the cycle and have exactly one directly attached leaf each. In the open arc from $a$ to $b$, suppose there are $k\ge1$ cycle vertices, all bare. Alternatively, allow $k=1$ and give its sole interior vertex one leaf and no other attachments. In the open arc from $b$ to $w$, suppose there are $g\ge1$ cycle vertices, all bare. The graph fails EveryMinimumSupport. The three marked sites may have other attached trees, and the complementary arc from $w$ back to $a$ may have arbitrary attachments.

Write $v$ for the first vertex from $b$ toward $w$. Color the graph blue except for the interior of the first arc, the interior of the second arc after $v$, the leaf at $w$, and the optional leaf of the interior vertex in the first arc. Use the leaves at $a,b$ as $H$. After loss of both, the first arc and those two leaves remain white. Each boundary site has its own lost leaf and a white cycle neighbor. The optional interior leaf also remains white. A singleton loss in $H$ lets the opposite boundary fill the $k$ cycle vertices in $k$ rounds, and the missing leaf is then restored in round $k+1$. If the only interior vertex has a leaf, it fills in round two. The other arc advances independently from the initially blue $v$ and its blue predecessor $b$. Once its $g-1$ white cycle vertices turn blue, the leaf at $w$ is restored in round $g$. Hence the restricted deadline is $\max(k+1,g)$.

If $v$ is lost, neither $b$ nor the side of $w$ can start a force into its arc. The site $b$ sees $v$ and its neighbor toward $a$ white. At the far end $w$ sees its leaf and its neighbor toward $b$ white. The front from $a$ fills the first arc in $k$ rounds, and $b$ restores $v$ in round $k+1$. The second arc and the leaf at $w$ then take $g$ more rounds. The slow completion time is exactly $k+g+1$.

Every other singleton loss also finishes. If $a$ or $b$ is lost, its blue leaf restores it in round one. If a cycle vertex other than $a,b,v$ is lost, neither a neighbor on the $b$ side of the first arc nor $v$ is disturbed, so $b$ fills the first arc. The front from $v$ fills the second arc and can restore $w$ itself if needed. The optional interior leaf has two blue cycle neighbors at its site once the first arc is filled. The only possible remaining whites are the lost vertex and the leaf at $w$. This leaf has no twin, so any such pair fills. If a leaf at an unmarked site on the complementary arc is lost, its parent sees no other white vertex and restores it in round one. If an off-cycle vertex in another branch attached to $a,b,w$ is lost at depth one, it has a blue child that restores it in round one. Losses further from the cycle do not affect the ring and are restored once the ring and the marked leaves have filled. An off-cycle singleton loss in another branch along the complementary arc likewise leaves the ring fronts available. Thus $H$ is minimum and its restricted deadline is strictly smaller. In particular, this excludes every cycle with at least three singleton leaf sites forming an independent set and with no other attachments.


## Dense leaf sites and subdivision of blue edges

**Six consecutive attachment sites.** Suppose a cycle of length at least seven has consecutive vertices $a,d,b,t,w,y,x$ in that order. The sites $a,b$ each have a direct leaf, the sites $d,w$ each have exactly one direct leaf and no other attachment, and $t,y$ each have an attachment. Other attachments are arbitrary. Color all vertices blue except $d,w,y$ and the leaves at $d,w$. Take $H$ to consist of one leaf from each of $a,b$. Removing both leaves blocks both forces into $d$, which remains white together with its own leaf. With either member of $H$ lost alone, the other boundary restores $d$ in round one and its leaf and the lost leaf fill in round two. In parallel $t$ restores $w$ in round one and an attached neighbor restores $y$ in round one. The leaf of $w$ is restored in round two.

If $t$ is lost instead, its attached neighbor restores it in round one while $y$ is also restored in that round. The white $w$ has no blue neighbor in round one and turns blue in round two. Its leaf turns blue in round three. Loss of a vertex in an attachment at $t$ could obstruct the force from $t$ in round one, but $y$ is restored from its own attachment and can force $w$ in round two because $x$ is blue. Loss in an attachment at $y$ leaves $x$ able to restore $y$ in round one. If $a$ or $b$ is lost, its intact leaf restores it in round one. The other boundary can restore $d$, and both $t,y$ are subsequently available to complete the other interval. If any other blue vertex is lost, at least one of $a,b$ still starts the first interval and $t$ can start the second, with $y$ restored by its attachment. In these cases the two initially white leaves are restored after their sites and ring neighbors turn blue, and a sole remaining lost vertex is restored by connectivity. Thus $H$ is minimum and the two deadlines are two and three. In particular the cycle with one leaf at each vertex fails for every cycle length at least seven. The length-three case appears above. The three remaining lengths have explicit certificates in SHORT_CORONAS in verify_leaf_run_witness.py.

**Blue-edge subdivision.** In a connected graph with a budget-one certificate $(B,H,z)$ and $|H|=2$, suppose $x,y$ are adjacent vertices in $B\setminus(H\cup\{z\})$. Replace the edge $xy$ with a path, color every inserted vertex blue, and allow arbitrary new all-blue trees attached at those inserted vertices. If the replacement inserts exactly one vertex, suppose at least one of $x,y$ has all its old neighbors in $B$. The certificate carries over with exactly the same two deadlines.

Indeed $x,y$ stay blue in both restricted singleton losses, the double loss, the slow loss and the intact coloring. All old white-neighbor counts then agree with the original process, so the relevant times agree. A singleton loss at any other old vertex also leaves $x,y$ blue, and a loss at $x$ or $y$ is restored by an inserted blue neighbor in round one. If an inserted path vertex is lost, another inserted neighbor can restore it in round one when there are at least two inserted vertices. With only one inserted vertex the stated old-neighbor condition supplies the restoring force. Loss at a new tree vertex is restored from an adjacent new vertex in round one, since all new vertices and both old endpoints were blue. This proves that all singleton losses complete.

For exactly three consecutive singleton leaf sites and no other attachments, explicit certificates cover cycle lengths four, five and six. On the six-cycle, number the sites $0,1,2$ and the bare vertices $3,4,5$. The base witness in BASES in verify_blue_edge_lifts.py has the blue edge $45$, and all neighbors of $5$ are blue initially. Subdivision of $45$ covers every longer cycle with these three consecutive leaf sites.

**Two separated pairs of leaf sites.** Consider two adjacent pairs of singleton leaf sites. Suppose in one direction the pairs are separated by $k\ge1$ bare cycle vertices. On the complementary arc suppose the first and last interior vertices are bare and distinct. Attachments on that complementary arc between the two bare vertices are arbitrary. Contract the path between the two bare vertices to an edge $xy$ and use the blue-edge subdivision proposition in reverse as a construction, by first finding a certificate on the graph with that edge.

For $k=1$, the seven-cycle case in BASES of verify_blue_edge_lifts.py supplies the certificate and the blue edge. A six-cycle with one bare vertex in each gap is also in that table. For $k\ge2$, on the graph with the edge $xy$, number the cycle $0,1,\ldots,k+5$. The marked leaf sites are $0,1,k+2,k+3$, and $x=k+4$, $y=k+5$. Start with ring blues $0,2,3,4,x,y$ and the blue leaf at $0$. Take $H=\{2,4\}$ and the slow loss $z=3$. The endpoints $x,y$ are blue in all the required loss patterns and $y$ has no white neighbor initially.

When both members of $H$ are lost, $0$ and $x$ restore sites $1$ and $k+3$ in round one. Those sites then each see a white cycle neighbor and their own white leaf. Vertex $3$ is isolated among white ring neighbors. The process stalls. On loss of $2$ alone the pair $3,4$ restores $2$ in round one, and the right front from $4$ reaches site $k+2$ in $k-2$ rounds when $k\ge3$. If $k=2$, $4$ is already that site. The restored site $k+3$ can fill its leaf after the right front arrives. Site $1$ and the two right sites thus have their leaves by round $\max(2,k-1)$. On loss of $4$ alone the pair $2,3$ sends its right front from $3$ through the $k-1$ white vertices ending at site $k+2$. The last leaves fill in round $k$. On loss of $3$ instead, $0$ and $x$ again restore $1$ and $k+3$ in round one. Vertex $2$ then restores $3$ in round two. For $k\ge3$, the right front from $4$ starts in round three and reaches site $k+2$ in round $k$. The final leaves fill in round $k+1$. For $k=2$, the leaf at $4$ likewise waits for $3$ to turn blue and fills in round three.

The other singleton losses are $0$, its leaf, $x$, and $y$. The leaf restores a lost $0$ in round one. If that leaf is lost instead, $2$ restores $1$ in round one and $0$ can then restore its leaf. The vertex $y$ directly restores a lost $x$. If $y$ is lost, $2$ restores $1$ in round one and $0$ can restore $y$ in round two, freeing $x$ to restore the site $k+3$. In all four cases the retained consecutive run through $2,3,4$ and the restoration of $0,x,y$ allow both ends of the run and their attached leaves to fill. Thus $H$ is minimum, the two times are $k$ and $k+1$, and arbitrary blue subdivision along $xy$ preserves the gap.

## Complete classification of direct leaf attachments

**Theorem.** A finite simple connected unicyclic graph whose off-cycle vertices are all leaves adjacent to the cycle satisfies EveryMinimumSupport exactly when it is a pure cycle or is a triangle with one of the listed positive leaf patterns. For a triangle the positive patterns, up to permutation of the sites, are $(1,0,0)$, $(1,1,0)$, and patterns in which every positive count is at least two.

The positive direction and all triangle patterns were proved above. For a cycle of length at least four with an attachment, the twin theorem handles sibling leaves, so suppose every site has exactly one leaf. The sole-site and two-site theorems exclude one or two sites. For at least three sites with no bare vertices, the six-site construction and the short certificates exclude the graph. Otherwise partition the sites into maximal consecutive runs, separated by nonempty bare gaps. A singleton run has nonempty gaps on both sides, so the two successive interval result applies with this singleton as $b$. If a run has at least three sites and there are at least four sites in total, choose the last three sites of that run as $a,d,b$ and the first following site as $w$. The variant with a shallow interior vertex applies. Exactly three sites in a single run were handled by blue-edge subdivision and its short base cases.

The remaining runs each consist of exactly two sites. Choose two consecutive runs. If the exterior path has at least two bare boundary vertices, use the two-pair result. If it has only one, there are exactly two runs and one gap has length one. Use that gap as the first gap and the other as the exterior path. If both gaps have length one, use the six-cycle certificate. This exhausts the shallow shapes for every cycle length.

## Coverage of the classification

The pure-cycle and positive triangle arguments prove membership for all listed graphs. For exclusion, the twin theorem handles graphs with twins. The sole-site and two-site results handle those attachment counts. The triangle and four-cycle sections handle every placement on those cycle lengths. For longer cycles, graphs with at least two nonpath attachments admit the two-center obstruction. Graphs in which every site has a single path are covered by the path-interval arguments and the terminal extension criterion. Four or more sites with one nonpath attachment are handled by the exterior constructions and the short-fork verification. The final three-site case is covered by the bare-arc and adjacent-site reductions and the 670 fork bases. The exclusions give a minimum killing pair whose restricted worst time at budget one is strictly smaller than the unrestricted worst time.

## Relation to prior work

Theorem 2.8 and Propositions 2.10 and 2.12 supply the fort criterion, the multiplicity criterion for fault tolerance, and the characterization of two-vertex forts by twins. Proposition 5.7 of the first preprint version gives the one-fault propagation time $\lceil(n-3)/2\rceil$ of $C_n$ for $n\ge4$, with the optimization over minimum-cardinality one-fault-tolerant sets. Our cycle formula agrees in that case. Such a set has four initial blue vertices, and after one loss the three remaining seeds have just one active blue run and $n-3$ whites. The theorem here treats arbitrary forcing sets, every minimum killing support, and all budgets. The exclusion witnesses exhibit a strict time gap for a specified forcing set and a specified support. [Vertex Fault Tolerant Zero Forcing, v4, Section 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.07854v4#S2), [Fault Tolerant Zero Forcing, v1, Section 5](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.07854v1#S5).

Jacob's well-failed property concerns the sizes of maximal failed sets, equivalently of minimal forts. On $C_6$, the sets $\{0,3\}$ and $\{0,2,4\}$ are maximal independent sets. Since a set on a cycle forces exactly when it contains an edge, they are maximal failed sets of different sizes. Thus $C_6$ satisfies EveryMinimumSupport and fails the well-failed property. Fixed propagation time concerns constancy over all inclusion-minimal forcing sets. The quantified comparison here uses losses inside each forcing set and includes every budget. [Well-failed graphs](https://ajc.maths.uq.edu.au/pdf/93/ajc_v93_p359.pdf), [Zero forcing propagation time intervals and graphs with fixed propagation time](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.16335).

Connected zero forcing minimizes the size of a forcing set constrained to induce a connected subgraph. Both $C_4$ and $C_4$ with one attached leaf have ordinary and connected forcing number two. For the graph with a leaf, choose two adjacent cycle vertices including the leaf's parent. A single cycle vertex immediately stalls, and a singleton leaf stops at its parent. The two graphs have different EveryMinimumSupport values, so $(Z,Z_c)$ does not determine membership. [Complexity and Computation of Connected Zero Forcing](https://arxiv.org/html/1607.00658#S5.SS1).

The literature check found related methods and parameters. Mathematical priority for this exact quantified classification remains unconfirmed.

The sole-site, separated-sites, and two-site path proofs cover 192 of the 600 previously uncovered negative census members through order eleven. The common-center path arguments cover a further 55 after deduplicating overlaps. The all-blue tree and single-leaf extensions cover a further 209 and 114 respectively. Each of the last 30 is a triangle with at most one path per site and a path of length at least two, so the triangle-path theorem excludes them. Thus all 2,815 negative census members belong to the established families or are obtained by a certified extension from a smaller excluded graph. The paired-center, remote-path and successive-interval constructions directly cover 72 instances previously handled by the extension replays, and the direct-leaf theorem settles all shallow shapes at arbitrary order.
